\section{The claim, and why it needed correcting}

We had said, in conversation and in the paper workplan's statement of the result, that our networks
learn larger machines, proved correct at every length, than any reported: finite-state transducers of
100--169 states, where ``the extracted-machine literature stops at a few states''. Read as stated, the
claim is \textbf{false}. Two works report larger machines learned by gradient descent and checked
exactly:
\begin{itemize}
\item \citet{brandoit-2026-length-independent-state-tracking} learn the word problems of $S_4$ (24
  states), $A_5$ (60) and the Mathieu group $M_{11}$ (7{,}920), on all 5 of 5 seeds each. Every
  trained model's transition tables are enumerated from the initial joint index over every symbol, so
  the check is exhaustive and proves the model correct at every length. Paper~1's draft cites this
  work, but not its machines' sizes.
\item \citet{giles-1995-learning-class-large-finite-state-machines} learn finite memory machines of
  256 and 512 states with a recurrent network that has no hidden state. At about 250 training strings
  the 512-state machine is learned with zero error on the complete set of 2{,}046 strings that
  identifies it, and the extracted machine is minimised.
\end{itemize}
What separates both from our largest results is \emph{how much of the state the training signal
tells the network}, not the size. The corrected claim therefore needs that variable.

\section{Terms}

A network is \emph{told the state} when its training signal determines the target's state at every
step. That happens in three ways:
\begin{enumerate}
\item it is trained on the state itself, as with \citeauthor{brandoit-2026-length-independent-state-tracking}'s
  labels (``the state supervised at every position'');
\item it is trained on outputs that jointly determine the state, as with division given its
  cover, whose sum and difference determine $(A \bmod p, B \bmod p)$ for odd $p$; or
\item the state is a function of a bounded window of past inputs and outputs that the network
  observes, as in a finite memory machine whose past outputs are fed back by teacher forcing
  \citep{giles-1995-learning-class-large-finite-state-machines}.
\end{enumerate}
Otherwise the state is \emph{hidden}: the network sees only outputs that leave most of the state
undetermined, so the outputs alone do not fix it. A machine is \emph{proved} when a complete
enumeration shows the network equal to the target on every input of every length. It is
\emph{tested} when it is checked on samples, test sets or bounded lengths, which can leave a wrong
network undetected (paper~1's K2: 2 of 105 addition networks passed every sampled test and are wrong).

Our runs give a state bound but no structure: one cell for every target, its codes at least three
times the target's states. On division and T11, the cell's two factors match the two coordinates
given.

Two of these call for care. \citeauthor{giles-1995-learning-class-large-finite-state-machines}'s
network taps are set to the machine's input and output orders (5 and 4), so the architecture is given

\citeauthor{brandoit-2026-length-independent-state-tracking}'s heads are sized to the group's action
(on $M_{11}$, 4 heads of 11 attractor indices, each tracking where one point is sent), so the
architecture is given a factorisation. Both are larger than ours all the same, and both are learned
by gradient descent and checked exactly or completely.

\section{The claim, made strictly correct}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Hidden state (stands, with a smaller number).} Among the work we found, the largest
  machines proved equal to their targets at every length, after gradient descent on outputs that
  leave the state hidden, are ours: the $S_4$ word problem and move-to-front on 4 items at 24 states
  (4 of 20 runs each), and random machines at 12 states (3 of 20) and, primitive, at 16 (2 of 30
  runs). The largest proved before is about 5 states
  \citep{zeng-1993-self-clustering-recurrent-networks,aichernig-2024-learning-minimal-automata-rnn}.
  Larger machines have been learned with the state hidden, but they were only tested.
  \citet{liu-2022-transformers-shortcuts-automata} report LSTMs at 100\% test accuracy on $S_5$
  (120 states) when the network sees only the permutation's first element, and on a Dyck automaton
  with stack-top observations, in distribution and at the training length. DeepDFA reaches 30-state
  random DFAs from accept labels, tested to 150 symbols
  \citep{umili-2024-deepdfa-automata-learning-probabilistic-relaxations}. So the claim stands only
  with ``proved'' in it. Our size is about five times the proved prior, at low rates: this is
  existence, not reliability.
\item \textbf{Told the state (no size claim).} With outputs that determine the state, our largest is
  division at 169 states (15 of 20). That is smaller than
  \citeauthor{giles-1995-learning-class-large-finite-state-machines}'s 512 and well below
  \citeauthor{brandoit-2026-length-independent-state-tracking}'s 7{,}920, so no size record is claimed
  here. What is ours is the controlled comparison: the same target and cell certify 16 of 20 told the
  state's coordinates and 0 of 20 on the quotient alone (121 states, Fisher $p = 7.7 \times
  10^{-8}$). The suffix control (state-carrying, no approximation, 0 of 20) says an approximation
  helps for being one, not merely for carrying state.
\item \textbf{Length.} Proof at every length is not ours alone:
  \citeauthor{brandoit-2026-length-independent-state-tracking} prove it too, by the same kind of
  enumeration. Ours applies it to Mealy machines whose outputs are not the state, with near ties
  branched both ways, and (K9) without the target in the loop.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Re-interpretation}

The correction moves the paper's weight from size to signal.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The 169 states are a result about approximations, not about size.} Division at 169
  states is learned when the network is told the state's coordinates. Told the quotient alone, it
  learns division at 25 states once in 20 and at 121 never. Paper~1's K4 and K6 read correctly as
  ``given its coordinates, a product machine of up to 169 states is learned reliably; without them,
  not at all''. That is a claim about what the signal must carry, and it is the one our data
  supports.
\item \textbf{The hidden-state frontier is about 24 states, and it is where the open problem is.} The
  algebras and random machines (T5, T10's P0) are the results nearest a ``larger than reported''
  claim. Their rates are 0--4 of 20, and random machines stop between 12 and 16 states. The
  machine-discovery aim (an unknown machine found from behaviour) lives here, where nothing tells the
  network the state.
\item \textbf{Approximations between the two.} The middle rows of Table~\ref{tab:ours} (the sum
  alone, nearly telling; T10's planted quotient, partly telling) are where approximations matter
  without handing over the state. T10b, P2 against P0 (8 against 2 machines, $p = 0.0206$, no
  margin), is the one registered test of an approximation that leaves the state partly hidden; T10r
  replicates it. T11 gives pairs that determine the state, so its rates, whatever they are, belong in
  the ``told'' class.
\item \textbf{What still has no precedent we found} is the reading of a trained network's
  decomposition against the target's congruences (K8), and the proof without the target in the loop
  (K9). Neither depends on a size record.
\end{itemize}

\section{Consequences for paper 1}

The first author asked for these to be applied. The draft now carries them, with the review's
additions (Section~\ref{sec:review}) and the contrast with
\citeauthor{brandoit-2026-length-independent-state-tracking}'s design for the discussion
(Section~\ref{sec:nfsm}).

\begin{enumerate}
\item The paper workplan's ``result nobody else has'' (100--169 states, learned and proved) does not
  stand as worded. It should be restated as the controlled effect of the approximations (K4, K6), plus
  the hidden-state results at 24 states.
\item The abstract's ``up to 169 states'' should say the network was given the state's coordinates.
\item Related work should give \citeauthor{brandoit-2026-length-independent-state-tracking}'s sizes
  and supervision, and add \citet{giles-1995-learning-class-large-finite-state-machines},
  \citet{aichernig-2024-learning-minimal-automata-rnn} and DeepDFA's 30 tested states. The sentence
  ``the extracted-machine work stops at a few states'' is true only of hidden-state work with exact
  checks.
\item The introduction's question (``how large?'') should be asked per signal: how large when told
  the state, how large when not.
\end{enumerate}

\section{The targeted search and the general review}
\label{sec:review}

\paragraph{The targeted search.} The aim was to find any network trained on outputs that leave the
state hidden whose learned machine above 24 states was checked exactly. The queries covered:
\begin{itemize}
\item exact extraction from networks trained on labels, including random DFAs of 20--100 states;
\item differentiable and discrete-state automaton learning;
\item group word problems under partial observation;
\item Moore and Mealy machines learned from input--output sequences with discrete latent states;
\item certified length generalisation of learned automata.
\end{itemize}
None of them found such a work. The nearest results are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \citet{liu-2022-transformers-shortcuts-automata}'s partial observations (above), which are
  tested only;
\item extraction by queries from transformers on small languages
  \citep{adriaensen-2024-extracting-moore-machines-transformers}, by state merging
  \citep{merrill-2022-extracting-automata-state-merging} and by clustering
  \citep{muskardin-2023-rnn-hidden-states-semantic-ground-truth};
\item the journal version of state-regularised networks
  \citep{wang-2022-state-regularized-rnns-extract-automata}.
\end{itemize}
All of these work on machines of a few states. \citet{allchin-2026-reward-teach-state-hidden-automaton}
probe RL agents for hidden automata of at most 11 states (20 in mined instances), measuring by probes.

\paragraph{The general review.} The field divides along the two axes of this note, how much of the
state the training signal tells the network and how the result is checked.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Told, tested:} state tracking with the state labelled at every position
  \citep{liu-2022-transformers-shortcuts-automata,merrill-2024-illusion-of-state-ssm,grazzi-2024-state-tracking-linear-rnn-negative-eigenvalues},
  expert trajectories for arithmetic
  \citep{papazov-2025-exact-learning-arithmetic-differentiable-agents}. Group word problems up to
  $S_5$ and beyond are learned, and length generalisation is the usual failure
  \citep{howe-2026-automaton-underneath-householder-linear-rnn}.
\item \emph{Told, proved:} \citet{brandoit-2026-length-independent-state-tracking} (to 7{,}920
  states) and, for finite memory machines,
  \citet{giles-1995-learning-class-large-finite-state-machines} (512).
\item \emph{Hidden, tested:} \citet{liu-2022-transformers-shortcuts-automata}'s partial
  observations, DeepDFA, and induction of transducers from string pairs, whose built machines reach
  280 states with no target machine to compare
  \citep{ginn-2026-neural-induction-finite-state-transducers}.
\item \emph{Hidden, proved:} a few states before us
  \citep{zeng-1993-self-clustering-recurrent-networks,aichernig-2024-learning-minimal-automata-rnn};
  ours to 24.
\end{itemize}
The theory of which architectures can length-generalise on which machines is algebraic and recent
\citep{zetzsche-2026-length-generalization-transformers-compression}. It bounds what a model class
can represent, not what gradient descent finds.

\section{Why Brandoit et al.\ succeed on every seed}
\label{sec:nfsm}

\paragraph{Is it 100\%?} Yes, on what they ran. Every NFSM model, on all 8 algebraic tasks and all
5 seeds (42--46), passes the exhaustive table check: 40 of 40. The 5 seeds are few, though. The
Wilson 95\% interval for 5 of 5 is 0.57--1.00, and a method certifying 80\% of seeds, as ours does
on division given its coordinates (15--17 of 20), would show 5 of 5 a third of the time
($0.8^5 = 0.33$). A per-task rate cannot be told from ours on 5 seeds. Across their 40 runs, though,
the method is plainly reliable on its tasks.

\paragraph{The method.} One NFSM layer sits in a residual backbone of width 128: an embedding, an
MLP and gated MLPs with dropout. On the algebraic tasks there is no convolution, so the layer reads
the current token alone. A head with $d$ attractor indices computes from the token a $d \times d$
logit matrix $\theta_x$. Its state is a one-hot index, updated by the column-wise arg max, so each
symbol acts as a transition table $\sigma_x$ on the indices. It is trained through the arg max by a
straight-through Gumbel-softmax estimator. The loss is a cross-entropy on \emph{the state at every
position}, weighted 1 up to the first wrong position and decaying after it ($e^{-3(t - t^\star)}$),
plus a term that keeps the Gumbel sampling exploratory at the first error. Training uses AdamW at
$10^{-3}$, cosine-decayed, with batches of 256 sequences of length 64. It stops after 40 consecutive
perfect checks or $10^5$ steps, so up to about $2.6 \times 10^7$ sequences.

\paragraph{How it differs from ours.} Five differences, each making their problem easier than
ours, and the first two decisively so.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{The state is the label.} With the state supervised at every position, and the loss
  focused on the first error, every table entry $\sigma_x(k)$ gets its own direct gradient: the
  first error names one wrong transition. Learning reduces to filling $|\Sigma| \cdot |Q|$ entries
  from labelled examples. Our targets' outputs leave the state hidden, so the network must invent a
  state assignment consistent with the answers. That is the combinatorial search their supervision
  removes. Our nearest analogue is division given its coordinates, where we certify 15--17 of 20
  at 49--169 states.
\item \emph{The architecture is given the factorisation.} The heads' sizes are set per task to a
  known decomposition with no slack: $(3, 2)$ for $S_3$ (a point's image and the sign), $(4, 3, 2)$
  for $S_4$, $(5, 12)$ for $A_5$, and four heads of 11 for $M_{11}$ (four points' images). The joint
  indices equal the group's order on $S_3$, $S_4$ and $A_5$. Our cell is one for every target, with
  codes about 8.5 times the states and two factors, and no decomposition is given. The network has
  to find one (K8 reads what it finds).
\item \emph{The transition depends on the token alone.} Each head's table is a function of the
  symbol, so the model class is exactly the finite semiautomata on its indices, factored by heads.
  Every point in it is a machine, and an exact one is reached once each column's arg max is right.
  Our next code is computed by a transformer from the incoming codes and the window, and snapped to
  a learned code in a continuous space. Most of our failures happen there: codes merge, states are
  lost, near solutions collapse (K10).
\item \emph{The readout is the state.} The joint index maps to the state through a fixed inverse.
  Our targets are Mealy machines whose output is a coarse function of the state and the token.
\item \emph{The budget and the data.} Up to $10^5$ steps of 256 full-length sequences of 64 symbols,
  against our 600 epochs of $2^{15}$ examples on a curriculum of short lengths. Their stop needs 40
  consecutive perfect checks on fresh data; ours stops at the first certificate.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{What it means for us.} Their 5 of 5 is the reliability of a solver matched to its
problem. The state is given at every step, the factorisation is given in the architecture, and the
hypothesis class contains only machines. It is not evidence of a strong general solver for hidden
states, and they test none. Their method does not apply to our hidden-state targets as it stands:
it needs the state labels for its loss and the decomposition for its heads. Three of its parts were considered as options for our architecture and set aside by the first
author (27 October 2026) as unsuited to it and to the programme's direction:
\begin{itemize}
\item transitions as token-indexed tables on the codes, a different model class whose factors
  cannot read each other within a step;
\item a loss focused on the first wrong position, whose rationale needs the state labelled;
\item exploration kept alive at that position, which assumes the tables.
\end{itemize}
The comparison that would be fair is T11, which gives the algebras pairs of approximations that
determine the state (``told''), with our cell's two factors and no sizes given. Its reading is
pending.
