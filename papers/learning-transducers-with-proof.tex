\begin{abstract}
What must a recurrent network be told to learn a finite-state transducer exactly? We train it on a
target's answers and some of its approximations, its homomorphic images (the congruence quotients of
its states), as extra outputs. A complete certificate then proves every learned network equal to its
target at every length, so, given the target, one certified run settles a machine, and a rate is a
cost. Approximations cut it. On modular division at 121 states, \pn{k4.cover}{16} of
20 runs given a cover, the sum and the difference, learn the machine, against \pn{k4.quotient}{none}
given the modular quotient alone; given the cover, \pn{k6.rates}{17, 16 and 15} of 20 do so at 49, 121 and 169
states. Whether the help is the approximations' own or that of what they tell about the state, our
runs do not separate: the state itself, the finest approximation, did at least as well as the cover
(\pn{t12.state}{17} against \pn{t12.cover}{11} of 20, no difference detected at 20 seeds an arm).
Beyond arithmetic, a planted approximation of 4 blocks, which leaves the state undetermined, given,
helps on random machines of 16 states (\pn{t10r.p2-machines}{10} of 25 machines against \pn{t10r.p0-machines}{2}),
and $S_4$ and move-to-front-4 learn in \pn{t11.s4}{16} and \pn{t11.mtf4}{18} of 20 runs given two
approximations, against \pn{t11.s4-alone}{4} and \pn{t11.mtf4-alone}{4} on their answers alone. On division (exploratory) and $S_3$, a factor of the learned state that holds an approximation
holds one the network was given; elsewhere factors rarely hold them. Sampled tests overstate: they pass networks the certificate refutes.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A recurrent network trained on a finite-state machine's inputs and outputs can come to compute the
machine exactly. When it is told the machine's state, by a label at every step, it does so reliably
and at large sizes: recurrent networks so supervised generalise robustly under distribution shift on small regular languages
\citep{dan-2022-robust-generalization-regular-languages}, and a network whose heads each hold a
congruence of a group's decomposition, the state labelled, learns group word problems of up to 7{,}920 states on all 5 seeds, each model
proved correct at every length \citep{brandoit-2026-length-independent-state-tracking}. Told only the machine's answers, the machines whose trained network is itself proved equal to the
target are small: before us, 4 states shown explicitly
\citep{zeng-1993-self-clustering-recurrent-networks} and 5 in most runs once the architecture is
tuned to each target \citep{aichernig-2024-learning-minimal-automata-rnn}; 24 states here, at low rates
(Appendix~\ref{app:hidden}; ``told'', ``hidden'' and ``proved'' as Section~\ref{sec:method}
defines them).\footnote{Extraction can recover a larger machine than the network computes:
\citet{omlin-1996-extraction-rules-discrete-time-rnn} extract a random minimal DFA of 10 states
exactly from a network that itself errs on long strings.} Between the two lies a choice every
practitioner makes: which extra outputs to train on. Extra tasks help or don't, and which help has
been found by trial \citep{caruana-1997-multitask-learning,both-2025-joint-task-training-small-models}.

We give an algebraic answer for finite-state targets: train on the target's \emph{approximations},
its homomorphic images, the congruence quotients of its states, whose covers algebraic automata
theory studies
\citep{hartmanis-stearns-1966-algebraic-structure-theory,krohn-rhodes-1965-algebraic-theory-machines,egri-nagy-2024-covering-lemma-transformation-semigroups}.
Trained on the answers and on some of the target's approximations as extra outputs, a network learns
transducers it does not learn from the answers alone, though whether the help is their being
approximations or what they tell about the state our controls do not separate; and on division and $S_3$, where a factor of the learned state holds an approximation, it is one
the network was given. Every learned machine here is proved equal
to its target at every length by a complete certificate, so ``learned'' is exact, never sampled.

Our contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item Approximations make direct products of machines learnable, at scale (Section~\ref{sec:a1}): division at
  121 states certifies in \pn{k4.cover}{16} of 20 runs trained on its two coordinates and in
  \pn{k4.quotient}{none} trained on its answers alone; trained on them, \pn{k6.rates}{17, 16 and 15}
  of 20 runs certify at 49, 121 and 169 states.
\item What is and is not separated (Section~\ref{sec:a2}): the state itself did at least as well as
  the cover (\pn{t12.state}{17} against \pn{t12.cover}{11} of 20, no difference detected), and the
  extra outputs that tell about the state without being approximations (the suffix, \pn{k5.suffix}{0}
  of 20 against the sum's \pn{k5.sum}{6}; the product, none) are descriptive after Holm's
  adjustment, so whether the help is the approximations' own or their telling the state is open;
  ``What the outputs tell'' (Section~\ref{sec:method}) states how much of the state each arm's
  outputs determine. A coarse approximation, one that leaves the state undetermined, helps on random
  machines (Section~\ref{sec:a4}).
\item Beyond arithmetic (Sections~\ref{sec:a4} and~\ref{sec:a5}): on random machines, a planted
  approximation given as an output helps against primitive machines, while for the same
  approximation present but not given no help was detected; and on groups and resets.
\item The mechanism, read exactly (Section~\ref{sec:a6}): on division (exploratory) and $S_3$ (registered), where a factor of the learned state is an
  approximation of the target, it is one the network was given; elsewhere factors hold the given
  ones rarely, and the help does not need them to.
\item The instrument (Appendices~\ref{app:certificate} and~\ref{app:addition}): a snapped network is a
  Mealy machine and a complete certificate proves it equal to its target or gives a counterexample;
  sampled tests overstate, passing networks that are wrong.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Extra outputs and intermediate supervision.} Multitask learning uses related tasks'
signals as an inductive bias, a network ``eavesdropping'' on a feature another task makes easy to
learn \citep{caruana-1997-multitask-learning}; which tasks help is an empirical matter, joint
training lowering the size needed to learn modular addition or permutation products only with
``compatible'' tasks, and similar ones not helping
\citep{both-2025-joint-task-training-small-models}. Predicting a DFA's state at every step as an
auxiliary task makes recurrent networks generalise robustly under distribution shift on regular
languages, and an auxiliary signal that is no approximation, a saturating count of zeros, helps on
parity too \citep{dan-2022-robust-generalization-regular-languages}; intermediate sub-task results make an
unlearnable composite (bit-subset parity) learnable
\citep{wies-2023-sub-task-decomposition-sequence-tasks}; an auxiliary loss on $S_3$'s sign, beside
the state labelled at every step, makes language models compute the sign first, and models that learned that algorithm
length-generalised less well than those that did not
\citep{li-2025-how-language-models-track-state}: an approximation given, and worse length
generalisation; and an observation of the state alone (one element
of an $S_5$ permutation) suffices for an LSTM to track it at the training length
\citep{liu-2022-transformers-shortcuts-automata}. The Neural Finite-State Machine trains each head to hold a
congruence of the target, the state decoded from the heads jointly and labelled at every step: its
heads are trained on the coordinates of a cover, with the state
\citep{brandoit-2026-length-independent-state-tracking}. We found no work that trains on a target's
congruence quotients as extra outputs without its states. Our full cover determines the state up to
relabelling and is close to state supervision, and our runs do not separate the two
(Section~\ref{sec:a2}); a coarse approximation records less (Section~\ref{sec:a4}).

\paragraph{Decompositions of machines.} We take the terms approximation, cover and coordinates from
\citet{egri-nagy-2024-covering-lemma-transformation-semigroups}, for whom an approximation is the
target of a surjective morphism, which can lose information, and a cover an injective one; the
congruence quotients used here are one kind of approximation. Classically a congruence of a machine's
states is a partition with the substitution property, and two whose meet is trivial decompose the
machine in parallel \citep{hartmanis-stearns-1966-algebraic-structure-theory}, as division's sum and
difference do; the Krohn--Rhodes and holonomy decompositions build cascades from a machine's
structure
\citep{krohn-rhodes-1965-algebraic-theory-machines,egri-nagy-2015-computational-holonomy-decomposition}.
Classical learners use them too: a product automaton is learned by its components
\citep{moerman-2017-learning-product-automata}, a Mealy machine output by output, each output's
machine a congruence quotient of the target and together the whole
\citep{koenders-2024-output-decomposed-learning-mealy}, and an automaton's sample complexity is
bounded by a cascade's components \citep{ronca-2023-automata-cascades-expressivity-sample-complexity}.
Our extra outputs ask a network for such congruence quotients, chosen to cover the state. Which
machines an architecture can represent or length-generalise on is now characterised algebraically
\citep{yang-2026-algebraic-decomposition-transformer-length-generalization,shakerinava-2026-expressive-limits-diagonal-ssm-state-tracking,nowak-2026-algebraic-view-recurrent-language-models};
those results bound what a model class can represent, not what gradient descent finds.

\paragraph{Learned and extracted automata.} Reading a machine off a recurrent network goes back to
second-order networks \citep{giles-1992-learning-extracting-fsa-second-order-rnn}; hard-thresholding
the state during training makes the network itself a state machine
\citep{zeng-1993-self-clustering-recurrent-networks}, as do learned centroids
\citep{wang-2019-state-regularized-rnns}, straight-through bottlenecks
\citep{koul-2019-finite-state-recurrent-policies} and a state chosen by hardmax among $k$, its
softmax regularised towards one-hot in training \citep{aichernig-2024-learning-minimal-automata-rnn}.
Aichernig et al.\ retrain at each minimised machine's size until it stops falling, so the minimal size
need not be known, and check the machine they read off the training traces against the target up to
isomorphism; our certificate (Appendix~\ref{app:certificate}) enumerates every reachable state under
every input instead, so no trace need visit them. Partitioning a continuous state can return
illusory machines \citep{kolen-1993-fools-gold-extracting-fsm}; extraction by queries finds wrong
inputs for networks perfect on their tests \citep{weiss-2018-extracting-automata-queries-counterexamples};
recent extraction is judged statistically or by abstraction
\citep{hong-2025-register-automata-data-sequences,wickramasinghe-2026-extracting-dfa-hyperplane-partitioning}.
Learned machines' sizes depend on what the network is told (Appendix~\ref{app:hidden}). Grammatical
inference learns our targets from far fewer examples
\citep{angluin-1987-learning-regular-sets-queries-counterexamples,oncina-1993-learning-subsequential-transducers};
it is not our comparator: we ask what a neural sequence model, trained as such models are trained,
needs to be told.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Targets.} A target is a Mealy machine read one column at a time, its output asked at every
column. \emph{Division} at $p$ reads two base-3 numbers $A$ and $B$, most significant digit first, a
column's token $3a_i + b_i$, and outputs the running $(A \cdot B^{-1}) \bmod p$, or ``undefined''
while $B \equiv 0$; its minimal machine has $p^2$ states (25, 49, 121, 169 at $p = 5, 7, 11, 13$),
the direct product of two modular counters. The \emph{algebras}: the word problems of $S_3$ and $S_4$ (6 and
24 states), move-to-front on 4 items (24 states), and random machines of 12 and 16 states. The
\emph{planted} machines (T10) are random machines of 16 states whose states fall in 4 blocks of 4 that
a random 4-state machine moves between, the blocks their only nontrivial congruence; \emph{primitive}
ones have none. \emph{Addition}
reads three base-3 numbers, a column's token the triple of its digits (27 values), and outputs the
sum's digit at every column; its minimal machine is the 3-state carry automaton. Addition serves
Appendix~\ref{app:addition} only, with its own network.

\paragraph{Approximations.} An \emph{approximation} of a target is here a homomorphic image of its
transitions, outputs set aside: a quotient by a congruence of its states, a partition that every
token moves as a whole, though the answer need not be a function of the class (division's is no
function of its sum); in
\citet{egri-nagy-2024-covering-lemma-transformation-semigroups}'s terms, one kind of approximation. Division's include
the running sum $(A + B) \bmod p$ and difference $(A - B) \bmod p$; for odd $p$ the two together
determine the state, so their direct product \emph{covers} the target and they are its
\emph{coordinates}. Figure~\ref{fig:approximations} draws them. The modular quotient and the product $AB \bmod p$ are no approximations (the data's arm names call the modular quotient \texttt{quotient}). A planted
machine's blocks are its approximation; a primitive machine has none. A network trained
on approximations answers them on the same input through query tokens, one output layer for all.

\paragraph{What the outputs tell.} Without extra outputs every target here leaves its state
hidden. Division's modular quotient has $p + 1$ values for $p^2$ states. The word problems output the item
at position 1, and move-to-front the last item, so 4 values for 24 states. The random machines
output one of 4 labels. Given its cover, division is told its state's coordinates at every column.
Given the sum alone, the sum and the modular quotient together tell apart all but $p - 1$ states, those with modular quotient $-1$, which share one label: 22 of 25 classes at $p = 5$. The product $AB \bmod p$, of as
many values as the sum and no approximation, tells 10 classes beside the modular quotient. The state itself tells all 25; T1's suffix carries the state but
is no function of it, its outputs needing a machine of 125 states; the token tells nothing of it. The
algebras given their pairs (T11) are told their state, as division given its cover is. A planted machine given its
blocks is told 4 classes of its 16 states, the answers' 4 labels besides.

\paragraph{Told, hidden and proved.} A target's state is \emph{told} when what the network is given
up to a column, its inputs and its training targets, determines the state at that column: a label of
the state, outputs that determine it, or past outputs fed back as inputs; otherwise it is
\emph{hidden}. A learned machine is \emph{proved} when the machine the trained network itself
computes, as it runs, is shown equal to the target at every length. A machine extracted from a
network that does not itself compute it, by clustering a continuous state for instance, is a
surrogate, and proving it says nothing of the network.

\paragraph{The network.} (Figure~\ref{fig:network}.) At each column a \emph{window} holds the column's token and its query
tokens, embedded with a learned position and read by a causal transformer of 3 blocks (residual width
32, 2 heads, feed-forward 64), its output a softmax per token. The state is handed from column to
column by gated recurrent cells whose outputs are snapped to the nearest of $m$ learned codes,
straight-through, with VQ-VAE's codebook and commitment losses (commitment weight 0.25)
\citep{van-den-oord-2017-vq-vae-neural-discrete-representation}. The state is \emph{factored} in two:
each factor has its own learned pooling query over the window's last-block tokens, its own cell and
its own codebook, and a projection of both factors' codes is added before every block, so the
factors' codes together are the state, up to $m^2$ joint codes. Each factor is 32 wide with
$\lceil 32\sqrt{q}/11 \rceil$ codes for a $q$-state target (21, 32 and 38 at $p = 7, 11, 13$), so
$m^2$ is 8.5 to 11 times $q$ across the targets; the networks have 45{,}219 to 47{,}790 parameters, codebooks
included. Training \emph{forks} on a fall: within a curriculum step, an epoch whose screen falls
below 0.6 of the step's best returns to the step's best network, halves the step's rate and draws
fresh data, at most 4 times a step. These constants (the fork's 0.6 and 4, the code count's 32/11, and below
the snap ratio 1.05, the near threshold 0.95 and the 3 times surplus) were chosen, not derived. This
is the paper's cell (T2, T5, T10, T11). T1, T3 and T12 keep the cell of the approximation claim at
$p = 5$ that T1 and T3 extend (Table~\ref{tab:approximation}; T3's controls,
Appendix~\ref{app:controls}): two
factors of 16 codes, residual width 16, no fork, 12{,}310 parameters.

\paragraph{The certificate.} A snapped network is a Mealy machine whose states are its reachable joint
codes: only the snapped codes cross from one column to the next, so a column's output and next joint
code are functions of the joint code and the column's token. The certificate enumerates it: from the initial code, every window from every reachable joint
code is run through the network, its output and next joint code recorded, until no new code appears;
the resulting machine is minimised and compared with the target's, every output and every next state. It is complete: a pass proves the network equal to
the target on every input of every length, and a failure yields a counterexample. A network
is \emph{certified} with its own snaps and \emph{robustly certified} when every near tie (a snap
ratio below 1.05, the nearest code's distance against the next one's) is branched both ways, so that
the pass holds under either rounding. Every network counted as certified here passes both. As an
independent check, every certified network is long-tested on catalogues of sampled inputs at
128, 256 and 1{,}024 columns, far past any training length (Appendix~\ref{app:certificate}).

\paragraph{Training and feasibility.} A curriculum: step $j$ trains on lengths $1, \dots, j$ columns,
warm-started from step $j - 1$, and ends when its \emph{screen}, the share of answers right on
sampled inputs of its training lengths, is perfect, after 10 epochs without
improvement or at 60 epochs; epochs of $2^{15}$ examples, batches of 64, Adam
\citep{kingma-2015-adam} at a constant 0.003. At each step's end the network is certified, and a run
stops at its first certified step, its network saved; otherwise it ends when its epoch budget is
spent (600 epochs; 400 for T1, T3 and T12). A failed run came \emph{near} a solution when every
answer reached 0.95 on its screen at some diagnosis, and \emph{collapsed} when its codes in use fell
below half of the diagnosis before. Every cell is checked against its target before any run: at
least 3 times as many codes as the target has states (T1's suffix arm excepted, below), and a curriculum able to reach the exclusion
bound $m + q - 1$ for $m$ joint codes and $q$ states
by Moore's argument (his bound for two machines of $n$ states each is $2n - 1$;
\citealp[Theorem 7]{moore-1956-gedanken-experiments-sequential-machines}): two machines of at most $m$ and $q$
states that agree on every input up to that length agree on every input. No run came near the bound: the
certificate, not the training length, rules out memorising.

\paragraph{Statistics.} Every comparison at 20 fresh seeds an arm, or with machines as the unit (T10,
T10r), registered with its direction before its data; Fisher's exact test, one- or two-sided as
registered (T12 two-sided, the rest one-sided); Holm's adjustment over the paper's
registered family, its 12 tests (T1's two, T2b, T3's two, T10's two, T10r, T11's three and T12), read
at 0.05; Wilson 95\% intervals. A run counts as a success only when certified in both verdicts (with its own snaps, and
robustly) and every long-test case passed. The tests are numbered as registered: T1 and T12 (A2), T2
(A1, A3), T3 (Appendix~\ref{app:controls}), T4, T4$'$ and T11c (A6,
Appendix~\ref{app:decompositions}), T5 (Appendix~\ref{app:hidden}), T6 (Appendix~\ref{app:proof}),
T7 (Section~\ref{sec:reproducibility}), T10 and T10r (A4), and T11 (A5). Where the unit is the machine (T10), each arm's machines are counted by their
successful runs and compared by an exact one-sided rank-sum test over those counts. T11's arms
given approximations are compared with T5's runs on the answers alone, an earlier batch of the same
method.

\subsection{A6: which approximations the factors hold}
\label{sec:a6}

Read exactly, a factor's codes induce a partition of the target's states; the reading asks whether
that partition is a congruence, an approximation of the target, and which one (T4, registered
before the reading; the tables are in Appendix~\ref{app:decompositions}). On division the registered reading (T4c) was mixed by its rule, between its expectation that fewer
than a quarter of the factors decompose and K8's half (\pn{k8.division-first}{43 of 116} factors, then
\pn{k8.division-fresh}{24 of 96}): a
minority of division's factors decompose, \pn{k8.division-one-line}{every} one into exactly one
congruence. Read beside it (exploratory), in the networks trained on approximations, \pn{k8.division-trained-lines}{every} such
congruence is one of the trained lines, the sum or the difference; trained on the modular quotient alone,
\pn{k8.division-untrained}{1} factor holds an approximation of its own, $B$. And a network whose two factors hold both is a learned cover. Nobody told
the network to put its approximations in separate factors; where it does, it uses the ones it was
trained on, and a network trained on none can find one. Recurrent networks trained on group word problems likewise
pass through partitions into right cosets, congruences of the group's action, during training
\citep{zhang-2026-tracking-states-or-cosets}.

Beyond division the factors hold the given approximation less often. On $S_3$ (T11c, registered),
\pn{t11c.s3-parity}{4} of \pn{t11c.s3-networks}{5} certified networks have a factor that holds
exactly the sign, the approximation that no network trained on $S_3$'s answers alone held (Appendix~\ref{app:decompositions}).
On $S_4$, \pn{t11c.s4-places}{4} of \pn{t11c.s4-networks}{16} have a factor holding the given point's
place, \pn{t11c.s4-pairings}{1} the Klein quotient, and \pn{t11c.s4-pair}{1} both; on move-to-front-4,
\pn{t11c.mtf4-order}{1} and \pn{t11c.mtf4-ahead}{1} of \pn{t11c.mtf4-networks}{18} hold one of the
given approximations and \pn{t11c.mtf4-pair}{none} both; most of the others hold smaller orders, such as
the order of two items, approximations that were not given, as some of T12's state networks hold the
coordinate $A$ or $B$ (A2). On the planted machines (T4$'$, registered),
\pn{t4p.p2}{5} of \pn{t4p.p2-networks}{22} networks given the planted approximation have a factor
holding it (mixed by its registered rule: below the expected half, above refutation's 2), and
\pn{t4p.p1}{none} of the \pn{t4p.p1-networks}{3} not given it. So the help (A4, A5)
does not need a factor that holds the approximation: where the factors don't decompose, they hold
the state jointly.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Every success here is proved against its target, so for a given machine one certified run settles it, and a rate is a
cost, not a verdict: restarts, and epochs to a certificate. Read so, the method gives existence: one
architecture, in two cells, certifies arithmetic, groups, resets and random machines, some with nothing
given (Appendix~\ref{app:hidden}). Claim A is about the cost. Trained on the target's answers and
some of its approximations, the cell learns machines far more cheaply than from the answers alone:
division at 25 states in \pn{k4.claim-cover}{15} of 20 runs against \pn{k4.claim-quotient}{1}, and
at 121 states in \pn{k4.cover}{16} against \pn{k4.quotient}{none} (A1, A3); groups and
move-to-front (A5); random machines with an approximation planted in them (A4). Extra outputs that
tell about the state without being approximations, the suffix and the product, certified no more
often than the answers alone, though not significantly less than the sum after Holm's adjustment
(A2).
The claim has five limits.

First, the approximations must be known to be given. A discoverer of an unknown machine has its
answers and none of its congruence quotients: they must come from known structure, or be found first. A network
trained on the answers alone sometimes finds one in a factor (Appendix~\ref{app:decompositions}),
but how to find them is machine discovery's open problem: the paper shows what to ask for, not how
to find it.

Second, the proof needs a bound on the states. Without a target, the stop and Chow's test set
together are sound at the exact bound, but at 1.1 times it the stop came falsely in
\pn{k9.slack-false}{11} runs, \pn{k9.slack-refuted}{4} of them caught by the test set and the rest
out of its reach (Appendix~\ref{app:proof}).

Third, a cover tells the network its state's coordinates, and the help is not separated from that.
At the finest approximation, the state itself, no difference was detected against the cover
(\pn{t12.cover}{11} against \pn{t12.state}{17} of 20, A2), so the cover's help, at 25 states and at
scale (A1), is consistent with state supervision's; so is A5's, whose pairs determine the state too.
The registered evidence that a coarse approximation helps, one that leaves the state undetermined, is
a planted approximation of 4 blocks on random machines (T10r, A4). ``More approximations, easier'' is
the gradient seen at 25 states (the cover \pn{k4.claim-cover}{15} of 20, the sum
\pn{k5.claim-sum}{10}, none \pn{k4.claim-quotient}{1}), though never a registered comparison, and
nothing here shows it a gradient of approximations rather than of what they tell about the state (the
product, no approximation and telling less than the sum, none of 20;
Table~\ref{tab:approximation}). At 20 seeds an arm, not detecting a difference does not show there
is none.

Fourth, the breadth is bounded. Beyond arithmetic the evidence is one planted approximation on
random machines of 16 states (T10 and its replication), and two algebras of 24 states given a pair
of approximations (T11; $S_3$, of 6 states, undecided). With the state hidden, random machines
certify at 12 states and not at 16 (Appendix~\ref{app:hidden}). Where a factor holds an approximation on division (exploratory) and $S_3$, it is a given one;
elsewhere factors hold the given ones rarely (A6), and the help does not need them to.

Fifth, one method, in two cells, serves every target, and their rates are what that costs.
\citet{brandoit-2026-length-independent-state-tracking} succeed on all 5 seeds at far larger groups,
with the state labelled and each head trained on a congruence of a known decomposition; ours is matched to no problem,
and none of its rates is every seed. And no certificate is silent: a run that has not certified yet
and one that never will look alike, so a machine's rate decides when to stop trying it. Rates here
are measured, not tuned: each test was registered before its runs, and a cell's failures are
reported with its successes.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:reproducibility}
No number in a table here was typed by hand. Each table is a function of the result files (the
runs' logs, their recertifications in both verdicts and their long tests), computed by
indrajala-ml's \texttt{scripts/paper\_tables.py}; \texttt{paper\_tables.py check} regenerates every
table whose label this manuscript holds and compares its rows with the text, and it reports them all
the same. Every measured number in the prose, the appendices' included, is held the
same way: each is marked in the source with its key, and the same script recomputes it from the
result files and fails on any difference; the numbers left unmarked are settings (lengths, bounds,
seeds). A run counts as a success only when it was certified in both verdicts and passed every
long-test case. Each test's result files are listed with their SHA-256 hashes in a manifest
beside them (\texttt{t1}, \texttt{t2-t5}, \texttt{t3}, \texttt{t4}, \texttt{t4-fresh},
\texttt{t4prime}, \texttt{t6}, \texttt{t6-proof}, \texttt{t6-strict}, \texttt{t7-reruns}, \texttt{t7-reruns-jebel},
\texttt{t10}, \texttt{t10r}, \texttt{t11}, \texttt{t11-runs}, \texttt{t12}, \texttt{t12-runs} and
\texttt{p2-check}, each
\texttt{.sha256}; the approximation claim's \texttt{approximation-claim.sha256}; and for addition's
studies, \texttt{matrix}, \texttt{control} (and its \texttt{models}), \texttt{certificates}, \texttt{long-tests},
\texttt{e3-e4}, \texttt{stable-replay} and \texttt{replay-guard}), and the datapack lists the
manifests' own hashes. Each verifies with \texttt{sha256sum -c} from the directory it sits in, but
\texttt{t2-t5.sha256} from \texttt{t2-t5/} and \texttt{t4-fresh.sha256} from
\texttt{t2-t5/approximations/}. Where a later re-read rewrote a report, the earlier manifest names the
original, kept beside it in \texttt{proof-loose/}.

Training is deterministic given the seed, the code and the native crate. Every run reported here ran
on x86-64. Before rerunning anything we fixed a rule for choosing the sample: the shortest certified
run of each of T1, T3, T2 and T5. These four were rerun on an aarch64 machine (a Raspberry Pi 5,
through the crate's NEON path) from the repository's current main. The expectation was bit identity:
every logged step the same but its wall-clock seconds, and the certified network's SHA-256 the
original's. \texttt{d5-sum-319} (T1), \texttt{approximation.d5-family-wide-319} (T3),
\texttt{s3-302} (T5) and \texttt{d7-family-309} (T2) all matched bit for bit, so training
reproduces exactly across the two architectures on these four tests' code paths: both cells,
division and an algebra.

The code (indrajala-ml, its native crate indrajala-math-rust, and this repository), the result
files with their manifests, and the literature we cite (where copyright allows) are public; the
datapack is archived on Zenodo.

\section{Addition: sampled tests overstate, and training past a proof}
\label{app:addition}

\paragraph{The network.} Addition's network is the paper's with one unfactored state: a number of
$n$ columns is read lowest column first in windows of $k$ columns, each window's tokens embedded
with a learned position and read by a causal transformer of 3 blocks (width 16, 2 heads,
feed-forward 32), a projection of the incoming state added before every block and a softmax per
token giving the window's $k$ digits. One learned query pools the last block's tokens, a GRU cell
(its update gate biased to keep the state at initialisation) updates the state of width $s$, and
the result is snapped to the nearest of $m$ codes as in the method. Training follows a curriculum
to 3 windows (3, 6 and 12 columns at $k = 1, 2, 4$); \emph{works} means every sampled test passes at
every length to 64 columns, and the long tests run to 1{,}024 columns (328{,}000 cases a network).

\paragraph{Sampled tests overstate.} On addition, \pn{addition.working}{57} of \pn{addition.networks}{105}
networks worked by their sampled tests to 64 columns; \pn{addition.certified}{52} were certified.
Of the \pn{addition.working-uncertified}{5} that were not, \pn{addition.wrong-passing}{2} passed every
sampled test and are wrong: one adds
$\pn{addition.counterexample}{1{,}559 + 6{,}329 + 5{,}231}$ to
$\pn{addition.counterexample-answer}{19{,}680}$ at \pn{addition.counterexample-columns}{12} columns,
and the other errs at \pn{addition.counterexample-other-columns}{12} columns yet passed all
\pn{addition.long-each}{328{,}000} long-test cases to 1{,}024 columns. In a registered comparison at
\pn{addition.codes-seeds}{20} seeds an arm, more codes raised the rate of networks passing the
sampled tests (Cochran--Armitage $p = \pn{addition.codes-working-p}{0.014}$, Holm
\pn{addition.codes-working-holm}{0.043}) but not the certified rate
($p = \pn{addition.codes-certified-p}{0.43}$): the extra codes buy networks that pass tests
without being the machine. That networks with perfect test accuracy can be wrong elsewhere is
\citet{weiss-2018-extracting-automata-queries-counterexamples}'s finding, made by an
automaton-learning search; here it is shown by complete enumeration.

\paragraph{The tests in detail.} A matrix of \pn{addition.matrix-runs}{90} runs
($k \in \{1, 2, 4\}$, $s \in \{4, 16\}$, $m \in \{4, 8, 16\}$, 5 seeds each) and a control of
\pn{addition.control-runs}{15} trained all at once at the same cells and seeds gave
\pn{addition.networks}{105} networks, every one put through the certificate. Of the
\pn{addition.failing}{48} failing networks, \pn{addition.failing-certified}{none} certifies. Of the
\pn{addition.working}{57} that work by the tests, \pn{addition.certified}{52} are certified and
\pn{addition.robust}{48} robustly; every certified network minimises to the
\pn{addition.minimised}{3}-state carry automaton, from \pn{addition.codes-reached}{3 to 11} codes
reached (median \pn{addition.codes-median}{5}). Of the \pn{addition.working-uncertified}{5} working
networks that do not certify, \pn{addition.break-early}{3} break before 64 columns, and
\pn{addition.wrong-passing}{2} passed every test to 64 columns and are wrong: one gives
$\pn{addition.counterexample}{1{,}559 + 6{,}329 + 5{,}231} = \pn{addition.counterexample-answer}{19{,}680}$
when the sum is read at \pn{addition.counterexample-columns}{12} columns (the sum is
\pn{addition.counterexample-sum}{13{,}119}), and the other errs at
\pn{addition.counterexample-other-columns}{12} columns. The long tests
(\pn{addition.long-cases}{19.35} million cases, \pn{addition.long-each}{328{,}000} a network) agree
with the certificate wherever they can: \pn{addition.long-certified-passing}{every} certified network
passes all of its cases, and \pn{addition.long-failing-rejected}{every} network failing a long test
is one the certificate rejects. They are also weaker: of the two counterexample networks, the first
fails \pn{addition.counterexample-long-wrong}{1} case in 328{,}000 and the other
\pn{addition.counterexample-other-long-wrong}{none}. The registered comparison of the code count
reran $k = 4$, $s = 4$ at \pn{addition.codes-seeds}{20} seeds for each of
$m$ = \pn{addition.codes-m}{3, 4, 6, 8 and 16}. The working rate rises with $m$ (Cochran--Armitage
$z = \pn{addition.codes-working-z}{2.45}$, $p = \pn{addition.codes-working-p}{0.014}$, Holm-adjusted
\pn{addition.codes-working-holm}{0.043} over that study's four registered tests), the certified rate
does not ($z = \pn{addition.codes-certified-z}{0.79}$, $p = \pn{addition.codes-certified-p}{0.43}$),
and the networks that work without being certified number
\pn{addition.codes-uncertified}{0, 1, 2, 4 and 5} of 20 from the fewest codes to the most.

\paragraph{Training past a proof.} This part is exploratory. Many of this paper's failures came near a solution first and lost it
(the tables' ``near'' columns; the sum's \pn{k5.sum-near}{11 of 14} in T1). On addition,
one proved network was replayed exactly from its saved network, optimiser state and generator. Read off after every batch, each loss of the proof
began as \pn{replay.first-pairs}{1 to 3} changed transitions, every one wrong, made by an update of
ordinary size, which in \pn{replay.silent}{8} of \pn{replay.count}{10} replays left its own batch's
loss below 1. The snap hides how near a state is to the next
code, so the change is a jump in the machine that the loss cannot see coming. Adam's second moments,
small after many batches at losses near zero, then turned the following spikes into updates five
times their median within \pn{replay.amplified-within}{1 to 13} batches in \pn{replay.amplified}{9}
replays, and the other states left their codes. A guard that undid
any update making a right machine wrong kept the proof in \pn{guard.kept}{10} of 10 replays, against \pn{replay.kept}{1} of 10 without it.
The guard is shown on one network's replays only: in a training screen (not registered) it did not
help, and it froze two networks' learning by undoing nearly every update, so no run here uses it.
Stopping at the first proof, as every run here does, avoids the mechanism rather than repairing it.

\paragraph{The replays in detail.} At $k = 1$, $s = 16$, $m = 16$, without the window's
transformer (the GRU does the whole step), a network certified at three successive curriculum steps
lost its proof in the next step's first epoch. From its saved network, optimiser state and
generator, \pn{replay.count}{10} replays of two epochs each lost the proof in \pn{replay.lost}{9}.
Reading the machine off after every batch, the first change from the certified machine was always
\pn{replay.first-pairs}{1 to 3} (state, window) pairs, every one wrong, made by an update of norm
\pn{replay.first-norms}{0.015--0.046}; in \pn{replay.silent}{8} of the 10 the batch's own loss did
not jump past 1. In \pn{replay.amplified}{9} the first update above 0.1 (five times the median)
came \pn{replay.amplified-within}{1 to 13} batches after that first change, in the last only
\pn{replay.amplified-last}{1{,}512} batches after it; at the worst,
\pn{replay.worst-wrong}{94 to 164} pairs were wrong. A certificate guard (registered before its data, the same
\pn{guard.count}{10} replays) undid any update that made a right machine wrong, restoring the
weights, the optimiser's state and the generator: it kept the proof in \pn{guard.kept}{10} of 10,
against \pn{replay.kept}{1} of 10 without it, undoing \pn{guard.undone}{174} of
\pn{guard.batches}{20{,}480} updates. Replays of \pn{stable.networks}{4} networks that kept their
proofs (\pn{stable.each}{5} each) kept them in \pn{stable.kept}{every} replay.

\section{The proof with no target in the loop}
\label{app:proof}

Chow's test set proves a black-box machine of at most a given number of states equal to a
specification \citep{chow-1978-testing-software-design-finite-state-machines}; complete test suites
have since been generalised to Mealy, Moore, weighted and nominal automata
\citep{kocsis-2024-complete-test-suites-monoidal-closed}. We use it to prove a learned machine equal
to an unknown target.

The certificate compares the network with the target. A discoverer has no target, only its answers:
without it, a machine that fits the data but is not the target's goes unnoticed, and
\citet{aichernig-2024-learning-minimal-automata-rnn} propose conformance testing against the system
in its place.
T6 (registered) asked whether the proof survives without one: train on answers alone, stop at the first
curriculum step whose answers are all right at its training lengths and at 24 and 48 columns and
whose machine, read off the network and minimised, has at most a bound's states, and then prove the machine equal to the black-box target with
\citet{chow-1978-testing-software-design-finite-state-machines}'s test set, which is complete for
targets of at most the bound's states. On \pn{k9.runs}{280} runs, at the exact bound (the target's state count),
\pn{k9.exact-false}{none} stopped falsely: every certified run stopped at its certified step, and
Chow's test set proved each of the \pn{k9.exact-proved}{80} machines (\pn{k9.exact-unproved}{none}
out of its reach; Table~\ref{tab:proof}). At 1.1 times the bound the stop came before the certified
step in \pn{k9.slack-early}{20} runs. In \pn{k9.slack-strict}{9} of them the stop's network was already
certified, though not robustly (a near tie), and Chow's test set proved the
\pn{k9.slack-strict-proved}{5} of those it reached. The other \pn{k9.slack-false}{11} were false stops,
their networks not certified: Chow's test set proved \pn{k9.slack-proved}{none} of them and refuted
\pn{k9.slack-refuted}{4} (Table~\ref{tab:refuted}), with counterexamples of
\pn{k9.counterexamples}{4 to 7} tokens; the other \pn{k9.slack-false-unreached}{7}, like
\pn{k9.slack-strict-unreached}{4} of the near ties and \pn{k9.slack-kept-unreached}{17} kept runs, were
out of its reach, since the test set grows exponentially in the slack
between the bound and the machine's states. It proved nothing false. So the proof needs no target in the loop when the size is known; when it is known only roughly, the
stop is unreliable and the proof must stay in the loop, where it caught every error it could reach.
