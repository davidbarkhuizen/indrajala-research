\begin{abstract}
\draftnote{Written last. Its points:
(1) Networks trained by gradient descent on input--output examples alone, never states or traces,
come to compute finite-state transducers whose outputs leave the state hidden. These are the word
problem of $S_4$ and move-to-front on 4 items (24 states) and random machines of 12 and 16 states.
Each is proved equal to its target at every length by a complete certificate. That is about five
times the largest hidden-state machine proved before, at low rates.
(2) Sampled tests overstate this.
(3) The snap makes the learned machine extrapolate.
(4) Given its approximations as extra outputs, a product machine of up to 169 states is learned
reliably (15--17 of 20). Without them it is not learned (0 of 20 at 121 states), and the
approximations help because they are approximations. When the approximations determine the state,
this is learning with the state's coordinates given: work that gives the state reaches larger
machines, so no size record is claimed for it.
(5) The learned factors, read exactly, hold the target's approximations.
(6) The proof needs no target in the loop.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

We searched the literature for neural automaton learning, extraction and verification, state
tracking and length generalisation, the 2025--2026 work last in October 2026. Two distinctions
order it. The first is how much of the target's state the training signal tells the network. The
state is \emph{told} when the network is trained on it, or on outputs that determine it, or when it
is a function of a bounded window of observed inputs and outputs. Otherwise it is \emph{hidden}.
The second is how the learned machine is checked: \emph{proved} equal to its target at every
length, or \emph{tested} on samples. Learned machines told the state have been proved at up to
7{,}920 states \citep{brandoit-2026-length-independent-state-tracking}. With the state hidden, we
found proved learned machines of a few states only
\citep{zeng-1993-self-clustering-recurrent-networks,aichernig-2024-learning-minimal-automata-rnn}.
Larger ones, to 120 states, have only been tested
\citep{liu-2022-transformers-shortcuts-automata}.

\paragraph{Learning automata and transducers.} Learning a finite-state machine from examples is
grammatical inference's problem. L* learns a regular set's minimal DFA from membership and
equivalence queries in time polynomial in its states
\citep{angluin-1987-learning-regular-sets-queries-counterexamples}, and OSTIA identifies
subsequential transducers in the limit from input--output pairs
\citep{oncina-1992-transducer-learning-pattern-recognition}. Either would learn our targets from far
fewer examples than we use. They are not our comparators: we ask whether a neural sequence model,
trained as such models are trained, comes to compute an exact machine, and how one would know.

\paragraph{Discretised states and automaton extraction.} Reading a machine off a recurrent network
goes back to second-order networks trained on regular languages
\citep{giles-1992-learning-extracting-fsa-second-order-rnn}; hard-thresholding the state during
training makes the network itself a state machine, which can be read off and minimised
\citep{zeng-1993-self-clustering-recurrent-networks}. Later work makes the state a mixture of
learned centroids \citep{wang-2019-state-regularized-rnns} or a straight-through quantised
bottleneck \citep{koul-2019-finite-state-recurrent-policies}. A state regularised towards one-hot
lets a network trained on input--output traces be read as a Mealy machine and checked for
isomorphism with the minimal target, at up to 5 states
\citep{aichernig-2024-learning-minimal-automata-rnn}. A probabilistic relaxation of a DFA learns
random DFAs of up to 30 states from accept labels, judged by test accuracy
\citep{umili-2024-deepdfa-automata-learning-probabilistic-relaxations}. A recurrent network with no
hidden state, its taps set to the target's input and output orders, learns finite memory machines
of 256 and 512 states \citep{giles-1995-learning-class-large-finite-state-machines}; there the state
is a window of observed inputs and outputs. Partitioning a continuous state can
return illusory machines \citep{kolen-1993-fools-gold-extracting-fsm}; a snapped state avoids this,
since its machine is the network. \citet{weiss-2018-extracting-automata-queries-counterexamples}
extract automata with L* and find misclassified inputs for networks at perfect train and test
accuracy (K2). Recent extraction is judged statistically or by abstraction: register automata
accepted under a PAC validation loop \citep{hong-2025-register-automata-data-sequences}, or L* with
a hyperplane partition of the state space answering equivalence queries
\citep{wickramasinghe-2026-extracting-dfa-hyperplane-partitioning}. Ours is a complete enumeration
against the target. ReLU and threshold networks can simulate any DFA, or probabilistic automaton,
exactly by construction \citep{dhayalkar-2025-neural-networks-universal-finite-state-machines,
dhayalkar-2025-symbolic-feedforward-probabilistic-automata}, but the trained versions are judged on
their data alone. \citet{brandoit-2026-length-independent-state-tracking} prove that tracking
state at every length under finite precision needs contraction and separation, which a snapped
state provides by construction. Their Neural Finite-State Machine learns the transition tables of
group word problems up to $A_5$ (60 states) and the Mathieu group $M_{11}$ (7{,}920), on every seed,
and an exhaustive check of the tables proves each model correct at every length. Three things set
their setting apart from ours:
\begin{itemize}
\item the state is supervised at every position;
\item each head's table depends on the current symbol alone;
\item the heads' sizes are set per task to a known decomposition of the group (on $S_4$, heads of 4,
  3 and 2 indices).
\end{itemize}
Our targets' outputs leave the state hidden, one cell serves every target, and the next state is
computed from the state and the window.

\paragraph{State tracking and length generalisation.} Transformers and state-space models trained on
state tracking often fail beyond their training lengths
\citep{deletang-2022-neural-networks-chomsky-hierarchy,merrill-2024-illusion-of-state-ssm}. Which
architectures can represent or length-generalise on which machines is now characterised
algebraically. The regular languages transformers length-generalise on exclude the Krohn--Rhodes
building blocks, the flip-flop and the simple groups
\citep{yang-2026-algebraic-decomposition-transformer-length-generalization}; diagonal state-space
models track a group only through a short subnormal series with abelian factors
\citep{shakerinava-2026-expressive-limits-diagonal-ssm-state-tracking}; and a recurrent model's
expressivity reduces to whether its syntactic monoid divides a wreath product under an explicit
arithmetic model \citep{nowak-2026-algebraic-view-recurrent-language-models}. Our algebras include
nonabelian groups ($S_3$, $S_4$) and the resets of move-to-front, which these results place at or past
the limits of transformers and shallow diagonal state-space models; a recurrence through a snapped
state reaches them, with a proof. Under partial observation, where the network sees one element of
an $S_5$ permutation or the top of a Dyck stack, LSTMs reach full test accuracy at the training
length \citep{liu-2022-transformers-shortcuts-automata}. That is a hidden state larger than ours,
tested rather than proved. On arithmetic,
\citet{papazov-2025-exact-learning-arithmetic-differentiable-agents} learn exact addition and
multiplication with differentiable finite-state transducers supervised on an expert's intermediate
steps, judged by tests; ours see only answers.

\paragraph{What a trained network holds.} Mechanistic work reads automata from transformers by
probing and sampled metrics \citep{zhang-2025-finite-state-automata-inside-transformers-cot}, or
builds circuits that simulate abelian-group automata
\citep{zhang-2025-partial-answer-transformers-learn-automata}. Language models trained to compose
permutations learn either an associative scan or a route that first computes the permutation's sign
\citep{li-2025-how-language-models-track-state}. None reads, exactly, the decomposition a trained
network holds against the target's congruences. The algebraic terms we use (approximation, cover,
coordinates) are those of the Krohn--Rhodes and holonomy decompositions
\citep{krohn-rhodes-1965-algebraic-theory-machines,egri-nagy-2015-computational-holonomy-decomposition,egri-nagy-2024-covering-lemma-transformation-semigroups};
on $S_3$ and $S_4$ our networks hold a point's stabiliser, never the sign that a normal series, or
those language models, go through (K8).

\paragraph{Auxiliary outputs.} Multitask learning uses related tasks' signals as an inductive bias,
a network ``eavesdropping'' on a feature another task makes easy to learn
\citep{caruana-1997-multitask-learning}. Supervising a recurrent network's states with the target
DFA's generalises far better on modulo languages
\citep{dan-2022-robust-generalization-regular-languages}. Our full cover determines the state up to
relabelling and is close to theirs: trained on it, the network is told the state's coordinates. One
approximation records less than the state. Intermediate
supervision of sub-task results can make an unlearnable composite learnable
\citep{wies-2023-sub-task-decomposition-sequence-tasks}. We supervise no steps: every extra output is a
function of the input read so far, and K5 shows that what helps is its being an approximation, not
its being an extra output.

\paragraph{Conformance testing.} Chow's test set proves a black-box machine of at most a given number
of states equal to a specification \citep{chow-1978-testing-software-design-finite-state-machines};
complete test suites have since been generalised to Mealy, Moore, weighted and nominal automata
\citep{kocsis-2024-complete-test-suites-monoidal-closed}. We use it to prove a learned machine
equal to an unknown target (K9).

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Targets.} A target is a Mealy machine read one column at a time, its output asked at every
column. \emph{Division} at $p$ reads two base-3 numbers $A$ and $B$, most significant digit first, a
column's token $3a_i + b_i$, and outputs the running $(A \cdot B^{-1}) \bmod p$, or ``undefined''
while $B \equiv 0$; its minimal machine has $p^2$ states (25, 49, 121, 169 at $p = 5, 7, 11, 13$),
the product of two modular counters. The \emph{algebras}: the word problems of $S_3$ and $S_4$ (6 and
24 states), move-to-front on 4 items (24 states), and random machines of 12 and 16 states. \emph{Addition}
reads three base-3 numbers, a column's token the triple of its digits (27 values), and outputs the
sum's digit at every column; its minimal machine is the 3-state carry automaton. Addition serves K2
and K10 only, with its own network (Appendix~\ref{app:addition}).

\paragraph{Approximations.} An \emph{approximation} of a target is a homomorphic image of it, a
quotient by a congruence of its states, as
\citet{egri-nagy-2024-covering-lemma-transformation-semigroups} use the term. Division's include
the running sum $(A + B) \bmod p$ and difference $(A - B) \bmod p$; for odd $p$ the two together
determine the state, so their product \emph{covers} the target and they are its
\emph{coordinates}. The quotient and the product $AB \bmod p$ are no approximations. A network trained
on approximations answers them on the same input through query tokens, one output layer for all.

\paragraph{What the outputs tell.} Without extra outputs every target here leaves its state
hidden. Division's quotient has $p + 1$ values for $p^2$ states. The word problems output the item
at position 1, and move-to-front the last item, so 4 values for 24 states. The random machines
output one of 4 labels. Given its cover, division is told its state's coordinates at every column.
Given the sum alone, the sum and the quotient together tell apart all but $p - 1$ states, those with
quotient $-1$, which share one label: 22 of 25 classes at $p = 5$.

\paragraph{The network.} At each column a \emph{window} holds the column's token and its query
tokens, embedded with a learned position and read by a causal transformer of 3 blocks (residual width
32, 2 heads, feed-forward 64), its output a softmax per token. The state is handed from column to
column by gated recurrent cells whose outputs are snapped to the nearest of $m$ learned codes,
straight-through, with VQ-VAE's codebook and commitment losses (commitment weight 0.25)
\citep{van-den-oord-2017-vq-vae-neural-discrete-representation}. The state is \emph{factored} in two:
each factor has its own learned pooling query over the window's last-block tokens, its own cell and
its own codebook, and a projection of both factors' codes is added before every block, so the
factors' codes together are the state, up to $m^2$ joint codes. Each factor is 32 wide with
$\lceil 32\sqrt{q}/11 \rceil$ codes for a $q$-state target (15, 21, 32 and 38 at $p = 5, 7, 11,
13$), so $m^2$ is about 8.5 times $q$; the networks have 45{,}219 to 47{,}790 parameters, codebooks
included. Training \emph{forks} on collapse: within a curriculum step, an epoch whose screen falls
below 0.6 of the step's best returns to the step's best network, halves the step's rate and draws
fresh data, at most 4 times a step. This is the paper's cell (T2, T5, T10, T11). T1 and T3 keep the
cell of the approximation claim at $p = 5$ that they extend (Table~\ref{tab:approximation}): two
factors of 16 codes, residual width 16, no fork, 12{,}310 parameters.

\paragraph{The certificate.} A snapped network is a Mealy machine whose states are its reachable joint
codes. The certificate enumerates it: from the initial code, every window from every reachable joint
code is run through the network, its output and next joint code recorded, until no new code appears;
the resulting machine is minimised and compared with the target's, every output and every next state. It is complete: a pass proves the network equal to
the target on every input of every length, and a failure yields a counterexample. A network
is \emph{certified} with its own snaps and \emph{robustly certified} when every near tie (a snap
ratio below 1.05, the nearest code's distance against the next one's) is branched both ways, so that
the pass holds under either rounding. Every network counted as certified here passes both. As an
independent check, every certified network is long-tested on uniform and stratified catalogues at
128, 256 and 1{,}024 columns, far past any training length.

\paragraph{Training and feasibility.} A curriculum: step $j$ trains on lengths $1, \dots, j$ columns,
warm-started from step $j - 1$, and ends when its screen is perfect, after 10 epochs without
improvement or at 60 epochs; epochs of $2^{15}$ examples, batches of 64, Adam
\citep{kingma-2015-adam} at a constant 0.003. At each step's end the network is certified, and a run
stops at its first certified step, its network saved; otherwise it ends when its epoch budget is
spent (600 epochs; 400 for T1 and T3). Every cell is checked against its target before any run: at
least 3 times as many codes as the target has states, and a curriculum able to reach the exclusion
bound $m + q - 1$ for $m$ joint codes and $q$ states
\citep{moore-1956-gedanken-experiments-sequential-machines}: two machines of at most $m$ and $q$
states that agree on every input up to that length agree on every input. No run came near the bound: the
certificate, not the training length, rules out memorising.

\paragraph{Statistics.} Every comparison at 20 fresh seeds an arm, registered with its direction
before its data; Fisher's exact test, one-sided as registered; Holm across the paper's family;
Wilson 95\% intervals. A run counts as a success only when certified in both verdicts and every
long-test case passed.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{K1: a snapped network is a machine, and a certificate proves it}
The certificate has run on \pn{k1.networks}{385} trained networks reported here: the
\pn{addition.networks}{105} of addition (Appendix~\ref{app:addition}) and the \pn{k1.coded}{280}
coded runs of T1, T2, T3 and T5. It has never disagreed with a long test. Here, all
\pn{k1.certified}{80} certified networks of T1, T2, T3 and T5 passed every one of their
\pn{k1.cases}{42{,}114{,}000} long-test cases (T1's \pn{k1.t1-networks}{7} networks,
\pn{k1.t1-cases}{2{,}625{,}000} cases; T3's \pn{k1.t3-networks}{13}, \pn{k1.t3-cases}{4{,}875{,}000};
T2's and T5's \pn{k1.t2-t5-networks}{60}, \pn{k1.t2-t5-cases}{34{,}614{,}000}). The only networks to
fail a long test were T3's \pn{k1.failing-uncoded}{20} with a continuous state, which have no
certificate; of the coded networks, \pn{k1.failing-coded}{none} failed a case. The long tests are the weaker check: they sample, and the certificate does not.

\subsection{K2: sampled tests overstate}
On addition (Appendix~\ref{app:addition}), \pn{addition.working}{57} of \pn{addition.networks}{105}
networks worked by their sampled tests to 64 columns; \pn{addition.certified}{52} were certified.
Of the \pn{addition.working-uncertified}{5} that were not, \pn{addition.wrong-passing}{2} passed every
sampled test and are wrong: one adds
$\pn{addition.counterexample}{1{,}559 + 6{,}329 + 5{,}231}$ to
$\pn{addition.counterexample-answer}{19{,}680}$ at \pn{addition.counterexample-columns}{12} columns,
and the other errs at \pn{addition.counterexample-other-columns}{12} columns yet passed all
\pn{addition.long-each}{328{,}000} long-test cases to 1{,}024 columns. In a registered comparison at
\pn{addition.codes-seeds}{20} seeds an arm, more codes raised the rate of networks passing the
sampled tests (Cochran--Armitage $p = \pn{addition.codes-working-p}{0.014}$, Holm
\pn{addition.codes-working-holm}{0.043}) but not the certified rate
($p = \pn{addition.codes-certified-p}{0.43}$): the extra codes buy networks that pass tests
without being the machine. That networks with perfect test accuracy can be wrong elsewhere is
\citet{weiss-2018-extracting-automata-queries-counterexamples}'s finding, made by an
automaton-learning search; here it is shown by complete enumeration.

stop is unreliable and the proof must stay in the loop, where it caught every error it could reach.

\subsection{K10: training past a proof can destroy it}
This section is exploratory. Most of this paper's failures came near a solution first and lost it
(the tables' ``near'' columns; the sum's \pn{k5.sum-near}{11 of 14} in T1). On addition (Appendix~\ref{app:addition}),
one proved network was replayed exactly from its saved network, optimiser state and generator. Read off after every batch, each loss of the proof
began as \pn{replay.first-pairs}{1 to 3} changed transitions, every one wrong, made by an update of
ordinary size, which in \pn{replay.silent}{8} of \pn{replay.count}{10} replays left its own batch's
loss below 1. The snap hides how near a state is to the next
code, so the change is a jump in the machine that the loss cannot see coming. Adam's second moments,
small after many batches at losses near zero, then turned the following spikes into updates five
times their median within \pn{replay.amplified-within}{1 to 13} batches in \pn{replay.amplified}{9}
replays, and the other states left their codes. A guard that undid
any update making a right machine wrong kept the proof in \pn{guard.kept}{10} of 10 replays, against \pn{replay.kept}{1} of 10 without it.
Stopping at the first proof, as every run here does, avoids the mechanism rather than repairing it.

\draftnote{Limits:
(1) The scale is learned with the state's coordinates given. Told the state, larger learned
machines have been proved \citep{brandoit-2026-length-independent-state-tracking}.
(2) With the state hidden, the frontier is 24 states at low rates; random machines stop between 12
and 16 states.
(3) The certificate is in the loop (K9 answers it offline).
The contrast with \citeauthor{brandoit-2026-length-independent-state-tracking}'s every-seed success
is the discussion's point. Their solver is matched to its problem: the state labelled at every step,
a loss focused on the first wrong position, tables indexed by the symbol alone, and heads sized to
the group's decomposition. Ours is one cell for every target, with the state hidden and the
decomposition found (K8). The hidden-state problem, where machine discovery lives, is the open one.}

\section{Reproducibility}
No number in a table here was typed by hand. Each table is a function of the result files (the
runs' logs, their recertifications in both verdicts and their long tests), computed by
indrajala-ml's \texttt{scripts/paper\_tables.py}; \texttt{paper\_tables.py check} regenerates every
table whose label this manuscript holds and compares its rows with the text, and it reports them all
the same. Every measured number in the results' prose and in Appendix~\ref{app:addition} is held the
same way: each is marked in the source with its key, and the same script recomputes it from the
result files and fails on any difference; the numbers left unmarked are settings (lengths, bounds,
seeds). A run counts as a success only when it was certified in both verdicts and passed every
long-test case. Each test's result files are listed with their SHA-256 hashes in a manifest
beside them (\texttt{t1.sha256}, \texttt{t2-t5.sha256}, \texttt{t3.sha256}, \texttt{t4.sha256},
\texttt{t4-fresh.sha256}, \texttt{t6.sha256}, \texttt{t7-reruns.sha256}; the approximation claim's
\texttt{approximation-claim.sha256}; and for addition's studies, \texttt{matrix}, \texttt{control},
\texttt{certificates}, \texttt{long-tests}, \texttt{e3-e4}, \texttt{stable-replay} and
\texttt{replay-guard}), and the datapack lists the manifests' own hashes.

Training is deterministic given the seed, the code and the native crate. Every run reported here ran
on x86-64. Before rerunning anything we fixed a rule for choosing the sample: the shortest certified
run of each of T1, T3, T2 and T5. These four were rerun on an aarch64 machine (a Raspberry Pi 5,
through the crate's NEON path) from the repository's current main. The expectation was bit identity:
every logged step the same but its wall-clock seconds, and the certified network's SHA-256 the
original's. \texttt{d5-sum-319} (T1), \texttt{approximation.d5-family-wide-319} (T3),
\texttt{s3-302} (T5) and \texttt{d7-family-309} (T2) all matched bit for bit, so training
reproduces exactly across the two architectures on these four tests' code paths: both cells,
division and an algebra.

The code (indrajala-ml, its native crate indrajala-math-rust, and this repository), the result
files with their manifests, and the literature we cite (where copyright allows) are public; the
datapack is archived on Zenodo.

\appendix
\section{Addition: tests against the certificate, and training past a proof}
\label{app:addition}

\paragraph{The network.} Addition's network is the paper's with one unfactored state: a number of
$n$ columns is read lowest column first in windows of $k$ columns, each window's tokens embedded
with a learned position and read by a causal transformer of 3 blocks (width 16, 2 heads,
feed-forward 32), a projection of the incoming state added before every block and a softmax per
token giving the window's $k$ digits. One learned query pools the last block's tokens, a GRU cell
(its update gate biased to keep the state at initialisation) updates the state of width $s$, and
the result is snapped to the nearest of $m$ codes as in the method. Training follows a curriculum
to 3 windows (3, 6 and 12 columns at $k = 1, 2, 4$); \emph{works} means every sampled test passes at
every length to 64 columns, and the long tests run to 1{,}024 columns (328{,}000 cases a network).

\paragraph{Tests against the certificate (K2).} A matrix of \pn{addition.matrix-runs}{90} runs
($k \in \{1, 2, 4\}$, $s \in \{4, 16\}$, $m \in \{4, 8, 16\}$, 5 seeds each) and a control of
\pn{addition.control-runs}{15} trained all at once at the same cells and seeds gave
\pn{addition.networks}{105} networks, every one put through the certificate. Of the
\pn{addition.failing}{48} failing networks, \pn{addition.failing-certified}{none} certifies. Of the
\pn{addition.working}{57} that work by the tests, \pn{addition.certified}{52} are certified and
\pn{addition.robust}{48} robustly; every certified network minimises to the
\pn{addition.minimised}{3}-state carry automaton, from \pn{addition.codes-reached}{3 to 11} codes
reached (median \pn{addition.codes-median}{5}). Of the \pn{addition.working-uncertified}{5} working
networks that do not certify, \pn{addition.break-early}{3} break before 64 columns, and
\pn{addition.wrong-passing}{2} passed every test to 64 columns and are wrong: one gives
$\pn{addition.counterexample}{1{,}559 + 6{,}329 + 5{,}231} = \pn{addition.counterexample-answer}{19{,}680}$
when the sum is read at \pn{addition.counterexample-columns}{12} columns (the sum is
\pn{addition.counterexample-sum}{13{,}119}), and the other errs at
\pn{addition.counterexample-other-columns}{12} columns. The long tests
(\pn{addition.long-cases}{19.35} million cases, \pn{addition.long-each}{328{,}000} a network) agree
with the certificate wherever they can: \pn{addition.long-certified-passing}{every} certified network
passes all of its cases, and \pn{addition.long-failing-rejected}{every} network failing a long test
is one the certificate rejects. They are also weaker: of the two counterexample networks, the first
fails \pn{addition.counterexample-long-wrong}{1} case in 328{,}000 and the other
\pn{addition.counterexample-other-long-wrong}{none}. The registered comparison of the code count
reran $k = 4$, $s = 4$ at \pn{addition.codes-seeds}{20} seeds for each of
$m$ = \pn{addition.codes-m}{3, 4, 6, 8 and 16}. The working rate rises with $m$ (Cochran--Armitage
$z = \pn{addition.codes-working-z}{2.45}$, $p = \pn{addition.codes-working-p}{0.014}$, Holm-adjusted
\pn{addition.codes-working-holm}{0.043} over that study's four registered tests), the certified rate
does not ($z = \pn{addition.codes-certified-z}{0.79}$, $p = \pn{addition.codes-certified-p}{0.43}$),
and the networks that work without being certified number
\pn{addition.codes-uncertified}{0, 1, 2, 4 and 5} of 20 from the fewest codes to the most.

\paragraph{Training past a proof (K10).} At $k = 1$, $s = 16$, $m = 16$, without the window's
transformer (the GRU does the whole step), a network certified at three successive curriculum steps
lost its proof in the next step's first epoch. From its saved network, optimiser state and
generator, \pn{replay.count}{10} replays of two epochs each lost the proof in \pn{replay.lost}{9}.
Reading the machine off after every batch, the first change from the certified machine was always
\pn{replay.first-pairs}{1 to 3} (state, window) pairs, every one wrong, made by an update of norm
\pn{replay.first-norms}{0.015--0.046}; in \pn{replay.silent}{8} of the 10 the batch's own loss did
not jump past 1. In \pn{replay.amplified}{9} the first update above 0.1 (five times the median)
came \pn{replay.amplified-within}{1 to 13} batches after that first change, in the last only
\pn{replay.amplified-last}{1{,}512} batches after it; at the worst,
\pn{replay.worst-wrong}{94 to 164} pairs were wrong. A certificate guard (registered before its data, the same
\pn{guard.count}{10} replays) undid any update that made a right machine wrong, restoring the
weights, the optimiser's state and the generator: it kept the proof in \pn{guard.kept}{10} of 10,
against \pn{replay.kept}{1} of 10 without it, undoing \pn{guard.undone}{174} of
\pn{guard.batches}{20{,}480} updates. Replays of \pn{stable.networks}{4} networks that kept their
proofs (\pn{stable.each}{5} each) kept them in \pn{stable.kept}{every} replay.
