\begin{abstract}
\draftnote{Written last. Its points: networks trained by gradient descent on input--output examples
alone (never states or traces) learn finite-state transducers of up to 169 states, each proved equal
to its target at every length by a complete certificate; sampled tests overstate this; the snap
makes the learned machine extrapolate; training on the target's approximations makes product
machines learnable (16 of 20 against 0 of 20 at 121 states), and because they are approximations;
the learned factors, read exactly, hold the target's approximations; and the proof needs no target
in the loop.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\draftnote{The question (D1): can a network trained by gradient descent on input--output examples
alone come to compute a finite-state transducer exactly at every length, how large, and how would

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

We searched the 2025--2026 literature in October 2026 for neural automaton learning, extraction and
verification, state tracking and length generalisation, extending the companion papers' searches
\citep{barkhuizen-2026-snapped-window-recurrence,barkhuizen-2026-certified-modular-division}. We
found no work that learns a transducer, or an automaton, by gradient descent from input--output
examples and proves it equal to its target at every length.

\paragraph{Learning automata and transducers.} Learning a finite-state machine from examples is
grammatical inference's problem. L* learns a regular set's minimal DFA from membership and
equivalence queries in time polynomial in its states
\citep{angluin-1987-learning-regular-sets-queries-counterexamples}, and OSTIA identifies
subsequential transducers in the limit from input--output pairs
\citep{oncina-1992-transducer-learning-pattern-recognition}. Either would learn our targets from far
fewer examples than we use. They are not our comparators: we ask whether a neural sequence model,
trained as such models are trained, comes to compute an exact machine, and how one would know.

\paragraph{Discretised states and automaton extraction.} Reading a machine off a recurrent network
goes back to second-order networks trained on regular languages
\citep{giles-1992-learning-extracting-fsa-second-order-rnn}; hard-thresholding the state during
training makes the network itself a state machine, which can be read off and minimised
\citep{zeng-1993-self-clustering-recurrent-networks}. Later work makes the state a mixture of
learned centroids \citep{wang-2019-state-regularized-rnns} or a straight-through quantised
bottleneck \citep{koul-2019-finite-state-recurrent-policies}. Partitioning a continuous state can
return illusory machines \citep{kolen-1993-fools-gold-extracting-fsm}; a snapped state avoids this,
since its machine is the network. \citet{weiss-2018-extracting-automata-queries-counterexamples}
extract automata with L* and find misclassified inputs for networks at perfect train and test
accuracy (K2). Recent extraction is judged statistically or by abstraction: register automata
accepted under a PAC validation loop \citep{hong-2025-register-automata-data-sequences}, or L* with
a hyperplane partition of the state space answering equivalence queries
\citep{wickramasinghe-2026-extracting-dfa-hyperplane-partitioning}. Ours is a complete enumeration
against the target. ReLU and threshold networks can simulate any DFA, or probabilistic automaton,
exactly by construction \citep{dhayalkar-2025-neural-networks-universal-finite-state-machines,
dhayalkar-2025-symbolic-feedforward-probabilistic-automata}, but the trained versions are judged on
their data alone. Certified transition tables for learned state trackers appear in
\citet{brandoit-2026-length-independent-state-tracking}, who prove that tracking state at every
length under finite precision needs contraction and separation, which a snapped state provides by
construction.

\paragraph{State tracking and length generalisation.} Transformers and state-space models trained on
state tracking often fail beyond their training lengths
\citep{deletang-2022-neural-networks-chomsky-hierarchy,merrill-2024-illusion-of-state-ssm}. Which
architectures can represent or length-generalise on which machines is now characterised
algebraically. The regular languages transformers length-generalise on exclude the Krohn--Rhodes
building blocks, the flip-flop and the simple groups
\citep{yang-2026-algebraic-decomposition-transformer-length-generalization}; diagonal state-space
models track a group only through a short subnormal series with abelian factors
\citep{shakerinava-2026-expressive-limits-diagonal-ssm-state-tracking}; and a recurrent model's
expressivity reduces to whether its syntactic monoid divides a wreath product under an explicit
arithmetic model \citep{nowak-2026-algebraic-view-recurrent-language-models}. Our algebras include
nonabelian groups ($S_3$, $S_4$) and the resets of move-to-front, which these results place at or past
the limits of transformers and shallow diagonal state-space models; a recurrence through a snapped
state reaches them, with a proof. On arithmetic,
\citet{papazov-2025-exact-learning-arithmetic-differentiable-agents} learn exact addition and
multiplication with differentiable finite-state transducers supervised on an expert's intermediate
steps, judged by tests; ours see only answers.

\paragraph{What a trained network holds.} Mechanistic work reads automata from transformers by
probing and sampled metrics \citep{zhang-2025-finite-state-automata-inside-transformers-cot}, or
builds circuits that simulate abelian-group automata
\citep{zhang-2025-partial-answer-transformers-learn-automata}. Language models trained to compose
permutations learn either an associative scan or a route that first computes the permutation's sign
\citep{li-2025-how-language-models-track-state}. None reads, exactly, the decomposition a trained
network holds against the target's congruences. The algebraic terms we use (approximation, cover,
coordinates) are those of the Krohn--Rhodes and holonomy decompositions
\citep{krohn-rhodes-1965-algebraic-theory-machines,egri-nagy-2015-computational-holonomy-decomposition,egri-nagy-2024-covering-lemma-transformation-semigroups};
on $S_3$ and $S_4$ our networks hold a point's stabiliser, never the sign that a normal series, or
those language models, go through (K8).

\paragraph{Auxiliary outputs.} Multitask learning uses related tasks' signals as an inductive bias,
a network ``eavesdropping'' on a feature another task makes easy to learn
\citep{caruana-1997-multitask-learning}. Supervising a recurrent network's states with the target
DFA's generalises far better on modulo languages
\citep{dan-2022-robust-generalization-regular-languages}; our full cover determines the state up to
relabelling and is close to theirs, while one approximation records less than the state. Intermediate
supervision of sub-task results can make an unlearnable composite learnable
\citep{wies-2023-sub-task-decomposition-sequence-tasks}. We supervise no steps: every extra output is a
function of the input read so far, and K5 shows that what helps is its being an approximation, not
its being an extra output.

\paragraph{Conformance testing.} Chow's test set proves a black-box machine of at most a given number
of states equal to a specification \citep{chow-1978-testing-software-design-finite-state-machines};
complete test suites have since been generalised to Mealy, Moore, weighted and nominal automata
\citep{kocsis-2024-complete-test-suites-monoidal-closed}. We use it to prove a learned machine
equal to an unknown target (K9).

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Targets.} A target is a Mealy machine read one column at a time, its output asked at every
column. \emph{Division} at $p$ reads two base-3 numbers $A$ and $B$, most significant digit first, a
column's token $3a_i + b_i$, and outputs the running $(A \cdot B^{-1}) \bmod p$, or ``undefined''
while $B \equiv 0$; its minimal machine has $p^2$ states (25, 49, 121, 169 at $p = 5, 7, 11, 13$),
the product of two modular counters. The \emph{algebras}: the word problems of $S_3$ and $S_4$ (6 and
24 states), move-to-front on 4 items (24 states), and random machines of 12 and 16 states.

\paragraph{Approximations.} An \emph{approximation} of a target is a homomorphic image of it, a
quotient by a congruence of its states, as
\citet{egri-nagy-2024-covering-lemma-transformation-semigroups} use the term. Division's include
the running sum $(A + B) \bmod p$ and difference $(A - B) \bmod p$; for odd $p$ the two together
determine the state, so their product \emph{covers} the target and they are its
\emph{coordinates}. The quotient and the product $AB \bmod p$ are no approximations. A network trained
on approximations answers them on the same input through query tokens, one output layer for all.

\paragraph{The network.} At each column a \emph{window} holds the column's token and its query
tokens, embedded with a learned position and read by a causal transformer of 3 blocks (residual width
32, 2 heads, feed-forward 64), its output a softmax per token. The state is handed from column to
column by gated recurrent cells whose outputs are snapped to the nearest of $m$ learned codes,
straight-through, with VQ-VAE's codebook and commitment losses (commitment weight 0.25)
\citep{van-den-oord-2017-vq-vae-neural-discrete-representation}. The state is \emph{factored} in two:
each factor has its own learned pooling query over the window's last-block tokens, its own cell and
its own codebook, and a projection of both factors' codes is added before every block, so the
factors' codes together are the state, up to $m^2$ joint codes. Each factor is 32 wide with
$\lceil 32\sqrt{q}/11 \rceil$ codes for a $q$-state target (15, 21, 32 and 38 at $p = 5, 7, 11,
13$), so $m^2$ is about 8.5 times $q$; the networks have 45{,}219 to 47{,}790 parameters, codebooks
included. Training \emph{forks} on collapse: within a curriculum step, an epoch whose screen falls
below 0.6 of the step's best returns to the step's best network, halves the step's rate and draws
fresh data, at most 4 times a step. This is the paper's cell (T2, T5, T10, T11). T1 and T3 keep the
cell of the approximation claim they repeat \citep{barkhuizen-2026-certified-modular-division}: two
factors of 16 codes, residual width 16, no fork, 12{,}310 parameters.

\paragraph{The certificate.} A snapped network is a Mealy machine whose states are its reachable joint
codes. The certificate enumerates it: from the initial code, every window from every reachable joint
code is run through the network, its output and next joint code recorded, until no new code appears;
the resulting machine is minimised and compared with the target's, every output and every next state
\citep{barkhuizen-2026-snapped-window-recurrence}. It is complete: a pass proves the network equal to
the target on every input of every length, and a failure yields a counterexample. A network
is \emph{certified} with its own snaps and \emph{robustly certified} when every near tie (a snap
ratio below 1.05, the nearest code's distance against the next one's) is branched both ways, so that
the pass holds under either rounding. Every network counted as certified here passes both. As an
independent check, every certified network is long-tested on uniform and stratified catalogues at
128, 256 and 1{,}024 columns, far past any training length.

\paragraph{Training and feasibility.} A curriculum: step $j$ trains on lengths $1, \dots, j$ columns,
warm-started from step $j - 1$, and ends when its screen is perfect, after 10 epochs without
improvement or at 60 epochs; epochs of $2^{15}$ examples, batches of 64, Adam
\citep{kingma-2015-adam} at a constant 0.003. At each step's end the network is certified, and a run
stops at its first certified step, its network saved; otherwise it ends when its epoch budget is
spent (600 epochs; 400 for T1 and T3). Every cell is checked against its target before any run: at
least 3 times as many codes as the target has states, and a curriculum able to reach the exclusion
bound $m + q - 1$ for $m$ joint codes and $q$ states
\citep{moore-1956-gedanken-experiments-sequential-machines}: two machines of at most $m$ and $q$
states that agree on every input up to that length agree on every input. No run came near the bound: the
certificate, not the training length, rules out memorising.

\paragraph{Statistics.} Every comparison at 20 fresh seeds an arm, registered with its direction
before its data; Fisher's exact test, one-sided as registered; Holm across the paper's family;
Wilson 95\% intervals. A run counts as a success only when certified in both verdicts and every
long-test case passed.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{K1: a snapped network is a machine, and a certificate proves it}
The certificate has now run on over 1{,}000 trained networks: 724 in the companion paper on modular sums
\citep{barkhuizen-2026-snapped-window-recurrence} and the 280 coded runs of T1, T2, T3 and T5. It
has never disagreed with a long test. Here, all 80 certified networks of T1, T2, T3 and T5 passed every
one of their 42{,}114{,}000 long-test cases (T1's 7 networks, 2{,}625{,}000 cases; T3's 13,
4{,}875{,}000; T2's and T5's 60, 34{,}614{,}000), and every network a long test failed is one the
certificate rejected; no certified network failed a case. The long tests are the weaker check: they sample, and the certificate does not.

\subsection{K2: sampled tests overstate}
On modular sums \citep{barkhuizen-2026-snapped-window-recurrence}, 57 of 105 networks worked by their
sampled tests to 64 columns; 52 were certified. Two of the five that were not passed every sampled test
and are wrong: one adds $1559 + 6329 + 5231$ to $19{,}680$ at 8 columns, and the other errs at 12 columns
yet passed all 328{,}000 long-test cases to 1{,}024 columns. In a registered comparison at 20 seeds an
arm, more codes raised the rate of networks passing the sampled tests (Cochran--Armitage $p = 0.014$,
Holm 0.042) but not the certified rate ($p = 0.43$): the extra codes buy networks that pass tests
without being the machine. That networks with perfect test accuracy can be wrong elsewhere is
\citet{weiss-2018-extracting-automata-queries-counterexamples}'s finding, made by an
automaton-learning search; here it is shown by complete enumeration.

trained on.

The certificate compares the network with the target. A discoverer has no target, only its answers.
T6 (registered) asked whether the proof survives without one: train on answers alone, stop at the first
curriculum step whose answers are all right at its training lengths and at 24 and 48 columns and
whose machine, read off the network and minimised, has at most a bound's states, and then prove the machine equal to the black-box target with
\citet{chow-1978-testing-software-design-finite-state-machines}'s test set, which is complete for
targets of at most the bound's states. On 280 runs, at the exact bound (the target's state count),
there was no false stop: every certified run stopped at its certified step, and Chow's test set
proved each of the 80 machines (Table~\ref{tab:proof}). At 1.1 times the bound the stop came
before the certified step in 20 runs. Chow's test set proved 5 of those machines equal to the target
anyway and refuted 4 (Table~\ref{tab:refuted}), with counterexamples of 4 to 7 tokens; the other 11,
like 17 kept runs, were out of its reach, since the test set grows exponentially in the slack
between the bound and the machine's states. It proved nothing false. So the proof needs no target in the loop when the size is known; when it is known only roughly, the
stop is unreliable and the proof must stay in the loop, where it caught every error it could reach.

\subsection{K10: training past a proof can destroy it}
This section is exploratory. Every division run of the companion paper that reached or neared a
solution and kept training lost it \citep{barkhuizen-2026-certified-modular-division}, and most of
this paper's failures came near a solution first (the tables' ``near'' columns). On modular sums, one
proved network was replayed exactly from its saved network, optimiser state and generator
\citep{barkhuizen-2026-snapped-window-recurrence}. Read off after every batch, each loss of the proof
began as 1 to 3 changed transitions, every one wrong, made by an update of ordinary size, which in 8
of 10 replays left its own batch's loss unchanged. The snap hides how near a state is to the next
code, so the change is a jump in the machine that the loss cannot see coming. Adam's second moments,
small after many batches at losses near zero, then turned the following spikes into updates five
times their median within 1 to 13 batches, and the other states left their codes. A guard that undid
any update making a right machine wrong kept the proof in 10 of 10 replays, against 1 of 10 without it.
Stopping at the first proof, as every run here does, avoids the mechanism rather than repairing it.
