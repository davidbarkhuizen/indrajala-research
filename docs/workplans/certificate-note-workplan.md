have done in indrajala-research and indrajala-studies - i think we should write up a detailed

The note is the instrument behind paper 1 (`papers/learning-transducers-with-proof.tex`,
doi:10.5281/zenodo.23826655) and behind every certified network since. In
[contribution.md](../../contribution.md) it is row 1, "the strongest contribution". Paper 1 gives
it one paragraph (Method, "The certificate") and two appendices (A, the long tests; F, the proof with

[machine-discovery.md](../research-notes/machine-discovery.md), whose stages A-F all depend on the
certificate and its target-free form.

## Why (the verdict's evidence)

**What the literature has (reviewed today, on top of the review in
[window-recurrent-literature.md](../research-notes/window-recurrent-literature.md), whose 2025-2026
sweep found no exact all-length proof for a learned network's automaton beyond a few states):**

- *Extraction* builds a surrogate machine. That may be quantising a trained state (Giles et al.
  1992; Omlin & Giles 1996), L\* with the network as teacher (Weiss et al. 2018), PAC validation
  (Mayr & Yovine 2018; Hong et al. 2025) or a hyperplane abstraction (Wickramasinghe et al. 2026).
  Proving a surrogate says nothing about the network itself: Omlin & Giles's extracted machine is
  exact while the network errs.
- *Reading off a discretised network* is Zeng et al. 1993's, and Aichernig et al. 2024 check the
  machine read off training traces up to isomorphism. Neither enumerates every reachable state under
  every input, so states no trace visits go unchecked. Brandoit et al. 2026 prove their NFSM's
  transition tables, but there the state is labelled.
- *Neural-network verification* checks properties, not equivalence with a machine at every length.
  Jacoby, Barrett & Katz 2020 infer invariants for RNNs; Khmelnitsky et al. 2021 do property-directed
  verification through extracted automata. For quantised networks, Henzinger et al. 2021 verify
  bit-exactly with SMT and show it is PSPACE-hard; QVIP and EQV are later tools for the same
  problem. Jia & Rinard 2021 show that ignoring floating-point error makes verification unsound and
  exploitable.
- *Conformance testing*: Chow 1978 and Vasilevskii 1973 give the W-method, and Kocsis & Rot 2024
  generalise complete test suites. A test suite is complete only for implementations within a state
  bound.
- *Certifying computation*: a certifying algorithm (McConnell, Mehlhorn, Näher & Schweitzer 2011)
  emits a witness with its output that a simple checker verifies. Translation validation (Pnueli,
  Siegel & Singerman 1998) proves each run of a compiler, with an analyser that emits a proof and a
  small checker that checks it.

**What we have that they don't:** a design condition (a recurrent interface made finite by an
exact snap) under which a trained network *is* a Mealy machine. Its equivalence with a target is
then decided by enumerating every reachable joint code under every window: complete, with a
shortest counterexample, margins and near ties branched. That turns "did it learn the machine"
from an estimate into a decision. The certificate then served as an instrument, not only a verdict:

- stop at the first proof;
- the guard against collapse;
- exact factor readouts (which congruence each factor holds);
- a target-free near-tie count;
- the rate read as a cost (a certified run is a Las Vegas run);
- with Chow's test set and a state bound, a proof without the target in the loop (T6), together
  with where that proof fails (1.1 times the bound).

**Validation in hand:**

- 385 networks checked; the certificate never disagreed with a long test (80 certified networks,
  42,114,000 cases);
- E1's two networks that pass 100,000-case tests to 64 columns and are wrong at 12;
- T6's 280 runs;
- the guard's 10 of 10 replays;
- certified machines of 3 to 169 states.

**Who benefits:** work on automaton extraction and interpretability, which needs a ground truth
better than sampled agreement; state-tracking and length-generalisation studies, whose evaluations
sample and so overstate (E1); and neural-network verification, for which this is a case where
complete sequence-level equivalence is cheap because the architecture makes it so. The principle
carries over to any network with a finite cross-step interface, such as the discrete latents of
world models (DreamerV2) or VQ bottlenecks, which are now common.

**What the note must not claim:** that any part is first. Each part has a precedent (above, and
contribution.md's "Not ours"). The note's claim is the method as a whole, its exact conditions and
its use as an instrument, stated as "not found in our review".

## Decisions

- **D1. The verdict: write the note.**
  - (a) *Chosen.* A dedicated methodology note on the certificate, with results only where they
    justify or validate it. Pros:
    - it gives the strongest contribution a citeable statement of its own;
    - outsiders can adopt it without reading a paper about approximations;
    - it fills the gap the review found;
    - it is mostly writing and offline analysis, while `jebel` and `stone` run the context-length
      study.

    Cons: days of writing beside the post-paper retests (X), which wait anyway for machines; and it
    overlaps paper 1 (handled by D3).
  - (b) No note: paper 1's paragraph and appendices suffice. Pros: no cost. Cons: the method stays
    buried in a results paper; its soundness conditions, numerics and target-free form are never
    stated together; machine discovery has no foundation to cite.
  - (c) Fold it into a later discovery paper. Pros: one fewer document. Cons: it waits on stages
    A-C, months away, and a discovery paper needs the method stated first.
- **D2. The form: a research note, then a Zenodo preprint.**
  - (a) *Chosen.* `research-notes/certificate-method.tex` (the name may change at drafting), built
    through `make` like every document here, released on Zenodo as paper 1 was. Pros:
    - the established route, with CI building the note;
    - no deadline shapes it;
    - a workshop version can be cut from it later.

    Cons: no peer review.
  - (b) Straight to a workshop (LearnAut, or a verification-for-ML workshop such as FoMLAS or
    SAIV). Pros: review and an audience. Cons: page limits cut exactly the conditions and proofs
    that make it a method; and choosing a venue is the owner's call. **Not for now** (the owner, 2026-11-01: Zenodo is the route for the moment).
  - (c) A Markdown note only. Cons: not citeable, and not the owner's "tex/pdf".
- **D3. Self-contained.**
  - (a) *Chosen.* It cites paper 1, the datapack and external work only, as paper 1 does
    (paper 1's self-contained rule, the paper workplan's D22, extended). Pros: an outside reader can follow every citation; paper 1 is
    published, so citing it is allowed. Cons: findings that live only in our notes (the margin stop,
    restarts) must be restated with their data, or left out.
  - (b) Cite our notes too. Cons: unpublished, and reviewers can't check them.
- **D4. No new training.**
  - (a) *Chosen.* Validation from the released networks (the datapack's verify pack and the
    studies' `data/`), recertified, fault-injected and bounded offline. Pros:
    - no runner time while the context-length study holds `jebel` and `stone`;
    - methodology needs measurements of the method, not more learned machines;
    - it follows the "finish planned before planning" rule, since offline analyses are exempt.

    Cons: the networks are paper 1's families only (D9 widens this a little).
  - (b) New runs on new targets. Cons: results-oriented, against the owner's framing, and they
    compete with X.
- **D5. The witness: keep "certificate", and make it one exactly.** In McConnell et al.'s sense a
  certificate is a witness that a simple checker verifies. Today's `certify` returns a verdict and a
  summary, not the table.
  - (a) *Chosen.* `certify` emits the full machine: for every reachable state and window, the
    labels, next states, near-tie branches and margins, plus φ and the minimised partition. A
    separate small checker, `certificate_check.py`, verifies:
    - the table's closure (every next state is a row);
    - φ as a homomorphism onto the target;
    - the table's fidelity to the network, by re-running every entry through the forward pass, in
      parallel and in any order.

    Pros: the term then holds exactly; the trusted base shrinks to the checker plus the forward
    pass (the translation-validation split); and it is a durable tool, built into the work.

    Cons: the tables are large (169 states × 531,441 windows would be too big, so they are stored
    compressed or regenerated on demand: stage 1 measures this), and checking costs as many forward
    passes as the search, though it is much simpler.
  - (b) Rename it ("exhaustive equivalence check"). Cons: breaks with paper 1 and every record;
    loses the witness idea, which the target-free form needs anyway.
  - (c) Keep the term without a checker. Cons: a precise established term used loosely.
- **D6. The tie-branched verdict.** In ML, "certified robustness" means robustness to input
  perturbations, while paper 1's "robustly certified" means every near tie was branched both ways.
  - (a) *Chosen.* The note says *tie-branched certificate*, with a footnote mapping it to paper 1's
    term. Pros: no clash with an established term. Cons: two names across two documents.
  - (b) Keep "robust". Cons: verification readers would misread it.
- **D7. Sound numerics: an interval bound, as a stage.** Today's margins (snap ratio 1.05, logit
  lead 1e-6) are chosen constants. The proof is about the network as executed by a deterministic
  implementation (bit-identical on x86-64 and aarch64, paper 1 §7). Jia & Rinard show why that
  should be stated, and checked where it can be.
  - (a) *Chosen.* Interval (or affine) arithmetic through the array network's forward pass from
    each reachable code and window, bounding each snap distance and each logit under every IEEE
    rounding order (or a stated relative error). Read per network: the share of entries whose snap
    and labels the bound certifies outright, and the entries it can't. Pros: it turns a heuristic
    margin into a sound one where the margins allow it; it is the note's one new method component;
    and a negative result ("margins are thinner than a sound bound") still informs, given the
    literature's recorded thin margins. Cons: one to two days to build; and LayerNorm and softmax
    widen intervals, so it may certify little at 169 states.
  - (b) State the assumption only. Pros: free. Cons: the first objection a verification reader
    raises.
  - (c) Exact rational evaluation. Cons: no exact form for `exp` or `sqrt`.
- **D8. Fault injection, and the exact chance of a sampled hit.**
  - (a) *Chosen.* On each family's released certified networks, wrap the network in the
    `WindowMachine` stand-in and plant single-entry faults: one (state, window) entry's label or
    next state changed, with entries chosen from the stationary visit distribution's rarest, median
    and commonest. Read: the certificate's catch rate (expected 100%, with the shortest
    counterexample's length). Then compute exactly, from the target's Markov chain under the long
    tests' sampling distribution, the chance that paper 1's long-test catalogue (its lengths and
    case counts) visits each fault. Pros: it turns E1's two networks into a law (how far sampled
    tests overstate, entry by entry); it is exact, offline and cheap; and it tests completeness on
    known defects. Cons: planted faults are not learned ones, so E1's real networks stay the
    evidence that the case occurs.
  - (b) E1's networks only. Cons: two anecdotes.
- **D9. A second step family.**
  - (a) *Chosen.* E6's linear-window networks (the window without its transformer; at least 2 of 6
    certified the carry automaton), recertified under the checker, if their files are on disk
    (stage 0 checks). Otherwise the note states the transfer (any finite cross-step interface) as
    scope, not as a result. Pros: shows the method is not tied to the transformer step, at no
    training cost. Cons: one small machine.
  - (b) Train a snapped GRU or a VQ world model. Cons: new training (D4).
- **D10. Machines.** Re-certification, fault injection and the interval bound run on `klip` (on
  since 2026-10-31: all cores, at most 85 °C, ideally 80). Each job's memory is planned before any
  parallel launch (5.3 GB). `pyramidon` reads results only. `jebel` and `stone` stay on the
  context-length study.
- **D11. Numbers from data.** Every measured number is keyed with `\pn` and recomputed by
  `scripts/paper/certificate_note_tables.py check` (indrajala-studies) from the result files, with a
  SHA-256 manifest. Pros: paper 1's standard; no typed numbers. Cons: the script costs about half a
  day.
- **D12. Release.** Before release, fresh-context reviews like paper 1's: literature, reasoning,
  and consistency with the data. Their findings are corrected, then a tag (`certnote-v1`), the
  preprint and its datapack addendum (the emitted tables, the checker's reports, the fault and
  bound results). **Revised 2026-11-01: the owner gave the go.** "so for the moment all we can hope for
  is to self publish on zenodo - but if it's worth it, then let's do it". It holds once the reviews'
  findings are corrected; a review that finds the method unsound or not new stops the release and
  goes back to the owner. Making the repos public is already standing.

## The note's outline (methodology first)

1. **The problem.** Claims that a network "learned an automaton" rest on samples or on
   surrogates. The design principle: make the recurrent interface finite, and equivalence becomes
   decidable by enumeration.
2. **The setting.** Mealy machines; the window-recurrent network with a factored snapped state. The
   conditions under which the network *is* a Mealy machine:
   - C1, a finite cross-step interface;
   - C2, an exact snap (a code's forward pass is its own vector);
   - C3, no other channel between columns;
   - C4, deterministic evaluation.

   Then "told", "hidden" and "proved", and the network against a surrogate.
3. **The procedure.**
   - breadth-first search over windows from the start code;
   - the output and next-state tables, and φ fixed on first reach;
   - minimisation, then equivalence with the target;
   - alignment and the final window;
   - factored states.

   Soundness and completeness proved, and the shortest counterexample.
4. **Numerics.** The logit lead and the snap ratio; near ties branched (the tie-branched verdict);
   split ties; the interval bound (D7) and what it certifies. What exactly the proof is about.
5. **The witness.** The emitted table and the checker; the trusted base.
6. **Without the target.**
   - consistency with the observations;
   - known approximations checked as approximations;
   - minimality;
   - Chow's test set with a state bound, its growth, and T6's evidence (sound at the exact bound,
     false stops at 1.1 times).
7. **The certificate as an instrument.**
   - stop at the first proof;
   - the guard (and its harm in training);
   - factor readouts;
   - the target-free tie count;
   - rates as costs.
8. **Validation.**
   - the 385 networks and the long tests;
   - E1;
   - fault injection and sampled-hit chances (D8);
   - cost against reachable states × windows (stage 4);
   - the bound (D7);
   - the second step family (D9).
9. **Related work** (the review above, with each precedent's line drawn).
10. **Limits.**
    - the window alphabet grows as `|Σ|^k`;
    - without the target, a state bound is needed;
    - the trusted base;
    - one architecture family, plus D9;
    - every target is arithmetic or algebraic;
    - a certificate never says when to stop trying.

## Findings

### Stage 0, the inventory (2026-11-01)

Read by `scripts/certnote/inventory.py` (indrajala-studies #8) over `data/window-recurrent/`,
written to the gitignored `data/certificate-note/inventory.csv`.

- **What is on disk.** 2,385 certificate files for 777 distinct networks, counted by SHA-256
  (addition's oldest files carry no hash). Of those networks, 691 are certified tie-branched, 430
  strict, and 374 in both.
- **The families.** Addition (3 states; `k` 1, 2, 4), the modular sums (4, 5), division (25, 49,
  121, 169), move-to-front (6, 24, 120), $S_3$, $S_4$ and $S_5$ (6, 24, 120), random machines (8,
  12, 16) and planted ones (16). Every certified network minimises to its target's state count.
- **The cost is small, so D5's feasibility worry is gone.**
  - Every `k` 1 cell certifies in at most 2.4 s and 4,104 window evaluations. The most reachable
    joint codes is 456, for 169 states.
  - Addition at `k` 4 is the largest: up to 5,314,410 evaluations and 10 minutes.
  - So a full table is at most about 7.4 M entries (14 × 531,441). Stage 1 stores tables outright;
    no regenerate-on-demand witness is needed.
- **The tie branching carries weight.** In every larger family the smallest snap ratio is within
  1e-4 of 1. Tie-branched certificates have up to 430 near ties per network on division and up to
  118,464 on addition at `k` 4. This refines D7: a sound check keeps the branching and replaces only
  the constant 1.05, branching to every code whose bounded distance interval overlaps the nearest
  code's. The bound then decides what branches; it doesn't have to separate the codes.
- **C1-C4 against the code.**
  - C1: only the joint code crosses columns (`NetworkMachine` asserts every factor is snapped).
  - C2: `certify` gives the next window the codebook rows exactly (`step_states` builds `C` from
    `E`). The library's inference hands a snapped state on as its code's vector (`window_states`'
    docstring), but nothing tests that bitwise; stage 1 adds the check on full-sequence inference.
  - C3: a window's step sees only `(X, C)`.
  - C4: inference on a Rust network runs the crate's window program, with the per-layer path as its
    reference, so the trusted base includes that program. Paper 1 showed bit identity across x86-64
    and aarch64 on four runs.
- **Networks to map.** 903 rows name no network file that resolves: 793 older files have no
  `network` field, and 60 point to retired recertification directories. Stage 1 maps them by file
  stem.
- **E6 (D9).** `data/window-recurrent/e6-linear/` holds models and strict certificates only. Stage
  1 recertifies them tie-branched, so D9 (a) is available.
- **The literature (stage 0's last item).** Mayr & Yovine 2018 is held in indrajala-literature (CC
  BY on HAL, #1). Jacoby et al. 2020, Khmelnitsky et al. 2020, Jia & Rinard 2021, Henzinger et al.
  2021 and McConnell et al. 2011 are cited there, with their PDFs in the private repository (#35).
  Pnueli et al. 1998 and Vasilevskii 1973 have bib entries only.

### Stages 1-4 (2026-11-01 to 11-02)

The runs on `klip` (under the thermal guard, peak 62 C), read on `pyramidon` from the gitignored
`indrajala-studies/data/certificate-note/` (recertify/, faults/, bound/, cost.json, the 2.9 GB of
tables; `manifest.sha256`); every number below is a `\pn` key of
`scripts/paper/certificate_note_tables.py numbers` or `certificate_cost.py`'s reading.

- **Stage 1, the witness (studies #9).** 1,088 jobs over 685 networks (every `k` 1 cell, addition
  at `k` 2 and `k` 4; both snap ratios where certified at each), 301,381,245 table entries in all.
  - 1,077 certificates certify and every one is valid under the independent checker; the 11
    uncertified (E6's linear windows at ratio 1.05) are invalid under it. Checker and certifier
    agree on all 1,088.
  - Every stored certificate is reproduced; each family network is reproduced by exactly one
    encoding (none ambiguous); shuffled batches move no snap and leave every margin's bits equal.
  - The largest table, addition at `k` 4: 5,314,410 entries, 57 MB, checked in 4 minutes.
  - So D5 holds as planned: tables stored outright, checked by a program that shares no code with
    the certifier.
- **Stage 2, fault injection (studies #10).** 4,032 planted single-entry faults (a wrong label or a
  wrong next state, at a table's rarest, median and commonest entry) in 672 tables: the
  certificate catches all 4,032, each with a counterexample of at most 11 windows.
  - The long tests' exact chance of missing a fault: 34 faults are more likely missed than found,
    26 of them at addition `k` 4's rarest entries, 6 at division's rarest (11, 13 and 7; 330 rarest
    division faults in all), 2 at addition `k` 4's median; the worst, 0.99998. Common and median
    entries are otherwise missed with chances below 1e-1000.
  - So the claim that sampled tests overstate gets its mechanism: a fault at a rarely visited entry
    passes thousands of long cases.
- **Stage 3, the interval bound (studies #11; D7).** 620 tables (every `k` 1, every `k` 2, the
  smallest `k` 4 per code count), 7 unsupported (window branches: division 11 and 13,
  move-to-front 4 and 5, $S_5$), 8,814,470 entries. No float value lies outside its interval and no
  decision is contradicted. 99.1% of labels and 98.1% of snaps are proved for the real-valued
  function; 384 tables wholly.
  - Shallow windows are all sound: the modular sums, move-to-front 3 and 5, $S_5$; addition 296 of
    304 tables wholly (labels 100%, snaps 99.97%).
  - Deep windows aren't: division 5 snaps 37%, 7 4%, 11 0.3%, 13 0.02% (labels 99.9%, 82%, 42%,
    19%); $S_4$ snaps 53%, move-to-front 4 82%, the random machines 72%. First-order intervals
    widen about a thousandfold an attention block.
  - That thin result on the deep windows is what D13 answers.
- **Stage 4, cost (studies #12).** Certifying costs about 0.6 s per 1,000 entries on division and
  0.04-0.05 on addition's long tables, checking 0.1-0.2 and 0.035-0.045; a table takes 10-60 bytes
  an entry. Every `k` 1 network certifies in at most 3.1 s and checks in at most 0.64. The proof

### D13, the affine bound (2026-11-02; read 2026-11-05)

`scripts/certnote/certificate_affine.py` (studies #14): affine forms (Stolfi & de Figueiredo) through
the window and the recurrence cells, the decisions read on differences, the symbols capped (1,024 an
entry, the dropped coefficients into the remainder, still an enclosure). Its first test, on
`pyramidon`, ran out of memory (uncapped, a deep window's forms passed 1 GB each), hence the cap and

437 and 175); division 13 (2,799 entries): all 2,799 sound (interval: 475 and 0), the cap reached
3,150 times, 336 MB, 913 s.

Its run: every table the interval bound leaves undecided (229 of 613: division 5, 7, 11 and 13,
move-to-front 4, $S_3$, $S_4$, the random machines, 8 of addition's), 3 shards on `klip` under the
thermal guard, about 4 hours (started 10:26 on 2026-11-02, `wt-certnote-affine`). Tables the
interval bound already decides wholly aren't rerun: a table both bounds decide adds nothing, and

### Corrections made while drafting (2026-11-05)

Each was found by reading the draft and the findings above against the code and the result files.

- **The completeness proof was wrong as first drafted.** A wrong next state shows a difference on `uxv` or
  on `u'v` (`u'` the path that first reached the next code), not on `uxv` alone; the certifier's docstring
  already says "for one of them". And a failure of a margin or a branch alone disproves nothing. Theorem 1
  now says both. **The certifier still reports `uxv` only,** so its reported case can be a right word: left
  as it is, stated in the note, since changing it would change the stored certificates' JSON.
- **"First failure gives a shortest counterexample" holds only for a wrong label.** The note says so.
- **Stage 2's "a counterexample of at most 11 windows" counts the path to the failing entry,** without the
  distinguishing word. The note says "the first failing entry 1 to 11 windows from the start".
- **Stage 2's "common and median entries are otherwise missed with chances below 1e-1000" is false at `k` 4:**
  90 median and 114 commonest faults on addition `k` 4 are above that, the commonest's worst about 4e-8. The
  addition rows also use the machine families' catalogue, not addition's own; the note says so.
- **"Every certified network on disk (777)" was wrong.** The 777 are the hashed networks, certified or not.
  Rerun: every file recording a certified verdict (`plan` resolves all, 0 unresolved, its job list identical
